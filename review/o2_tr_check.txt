en_O01: Your loved one has come back from surgery and fallen asleep. The number on the monitor dropped, the machine beeped, and the nurse gave oxygen through small tubes in the nose. Let's calmly look at what is happening.

en_O02: This number is called oxygen saturation: it shows how much oxygen the blood is carrying. It is measured by a sensor on the finger. In most adults it is usually ninety-five percent or higher. The care team sets the target level for each patient: if there is a risk of carbon dioxide building up, it may be lower.

en_O03: Why does saturation drop during sleep? After anaesthesia and some painkillers, breathing can become slower and shallower. During sleep the airway can sometimes narrow or become blocked, especially in people who stop breathing in their sleep. Also, some areas of the lungs expand less well after anaesthesia. So when a person falls asleep, saturation may drop, even if it is higher while they are awake.

en_O04: After general anaesthesia this can happen even after minor operations. But a marked or repeated drop in saturation should not be considered normal. That is why staff check breathing, the patient's condition, and how easily the person wakes up.

en_O05: If saturation is low, the nurse checks the sensor and the patient, may wake them and ask them to take deep breaths, and if needed gives oxygen through a nasal cannula: two soft tubes in the nose. The flow is adjusted to keep saturation within the target range. Oxygen helps raise saturation, but it does not always fix the cause of the breathing problem. So the care team keeps checking breathing and the patient's condition.

en_O06: How you can help. Stay close and stay calm. Do not remove the nasal cannula or touch the oxygen control. Do not give medicines, food or drink without the nurse's permission. When the person is fully awake, they can take a few deep breaths and cough, unless the nurse has said otherwise.

en_O07: Sometimes the monitor beeps for reasons that are not about breathing: for example cold fingers, hand movement, or a sensor that has slipped. Do not switch the monitor off yourself. Call the nurse to check.

en_O08: Call staff immediately, with the call button or by calling out loudly, if the person is hard to wake, is breathing very slowly, with pauses or shallowly, if the lips or face turn blue, or if there is shortness of breath, chest pain or sudden confusion. Do not wait for the lips to turn blue or for saturation to drop: on oxygen the number can sometimes stay normal even when breathing is dangerously weak.

en_O09: Oxygen safety. No smoking nearby, including e-cigarettes, and no open flames. Do not put petroleum jelly or oily creams on the face or lips. If the lips are dry, ask the nurse which water-based product can be used. Make sure the oxygen tubing does not kink or tangle around the feet. If the patient needs to get up, call staff first.

en_O10: As recovery from anaesthesia continues, the need for oxygen often decreases. But if saturation drops again, staff look for the cause. Oxygen is stopped by the care team's decision, when the patient keeps the target level without it.

en_O11: This is general advice. Individual instructions from your doctor and care team take priority.
