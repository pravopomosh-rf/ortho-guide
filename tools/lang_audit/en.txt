## The Achilles tendon
- The Achilles tendon connects two calf muscles, the gastrocnemius and soleus, to the heel bone. It lets us stand on tiptoe, walk and run.
## The rupture
- A rupture often happens during a sudden push-off or a jump. It can feel as if someone kicked you in the back of the lower leg, sometimes closer to the heel. Sometimes there is a snap, and it is hard to stand on tiptoe.
## The operation
- During the operation, the surgeon stitches the ends of the tendon together. Sometimes a rupture is treated without surgery, in a cast or a boot. The treatment is chosen together with the surgeon.
## After surgery
- After surgery, the foot is held in a splint or boot, usually with the toes pointing down. This keeps the repair from stretching. Do not remove the splint or boot or change the position of your foot without permission and instructions from the medical team.
## Weight-bearing on the operated leg
- Put weight on the leg only as the surgeon allows. Crutches or a walker are usually used. Following the doctor's plan, the foot is gradually brought back towards its normal position. If the boot has wedges under the heel, they are removed on a set schedule. Do not do this yourself.
## Swelling
- For the first few days, keep your leg raised when sitting or lying down. Wiggle your toes if this is allowed.
## The wound
- Keep the dressing clean and dry. After this operation, wound healing problems can occur, so the wound needs to be watched carefully. Do not get it wet, and do not remove the dressing unless told to.
## When to get help
- See a doctor the same day if the dressing gets soaked, redness of the wound increases, or there is discharge or a fever. If there is severe pain or pressure under the boot, new numbness, or cold or pale toes, get medical help immediately.
## Blood clots
- When the leg is immobilised, the risk of blood clots goes up. If prevention has been prescribed, do not miss it. Report pain, swelling or redness in the calf urgently. If you suddenly feel short of breath or have chest pain, call the staff at once; at home, call one zero one.
## Re-rupture
- Do not stand on tiptoe or make sudden movements until you are allowed. If you suddenly feel a new snap or a blow to the back of the lower leg, or notice sudden weakness of the foot, stop putting weight on it and contact your surgeon urgently.
## Recovery
- Recovery takes months. A physiotherapist chooses your exercises. Do not smoke: smoking slows healing.

## Who is this page for
For patients after a planned or urgent orthopedic operation (joint replacement, surgery to treat a fracture, or spine surgery) and their families. Individual instructions from your surgeon, anesthesiologist, physical therapist, and nurses are more important than these general recommendations.Illustration: a ward bed, the call button, a walker, and the way home.
## The first hours after surgery
- Nurses regularly check your blood pressure, pulse, breathing, oxygen level (saturation), temperature, dressing, and feeling and movement in your leg or arm.
- After major hip, knee or spine surgery, patients in our ward stay in bed on the day of surgery until the medical team says it is safe to get up. Staff decide whether to raise the bed side rails, based on your condition and your risk of falling. Rails reduce the risk of accidentally falling out of bed, but they are not suitable for everyone and cannot prevent every fall. Do not try to climb over or lower the rails yourself, as you could fall. If you need help or need to use the toilet, press the call button. Staff will help you or bring you a urinal or bedpan.
- Tell the nurse if your pain suddenly gets much worse, if numbness or weakness in your arm or leg appears or gets worse, or if you have severe nausea, dizziness, difficulty breathing, or bleeding. Also tell the nurse if you cannot pass urine.
- If your hand or foot suddenly becomes cold, pale or bluish — especially together with severe pain, numbness or weakness — call the staff immediately.
- If the patient suddenly becomes confused, unusually sleepy, or stops recognizing family members, relatives must tell the staff right away.
- You start drinking and eating when the medical team allows it.
More: Confusion after surgery (delirium)
## Pain
- To reduce pain, several pain medicines may be combined, for example paracetamol and, if needed, opioids. Anti-inflammatory medicines are not suitable for everyone: whether they are used depends on the type of surgery, the risk of bleeding, kidney function, and other medical conditions. The goal of pain relief is to reduce pain enough for you to move safely, rest, and do your prescribed exercises (NICE NG180; NICE CG124).
- Do not put up with severe pain — tell the nurse. Do not take any of your own pain medicines without approval from your medical team.
- Besides medicines, a comfortable position of the operated leg or arm and cold applied over a cloth may help — only as the staff allow; the dressing must stay dry.
- Opioids can cause sleepiness, nausea, and constipation, and sometimes dangerously slowed breathing. They are taken exactly as prescribed and only for as long as they are needed. If the person is hard to wake up or their breathing has become slow and shallow, call the medical staff immediately (FDA).
More: Pain after surgery: pain relief
## Getting up for the first time and falls
- It is important to start moving as early as possible after surgery, when the medical team allows it. After a planned joint replacement, rehabilitation usually starts on the day of surgery or within the first 24 hours (NICE NG157). In our ward, after major surgery you stay in bed on the day of the operation, so the first time you get up is usually the next day — with a physiotherapist or nurse. After surgery for a hip fracture, it usually starts the next day (NICE CG124). After other operations, the timing and how much weight you may put on the limb are decided individually. The first time you get up, staff will help you.
- The first time, do not get up on your own: press the call button (call bell) and wait for staff. You may feel dizzy and weak.
- To avoid falling: comfortable, closed, non-slip shoes, your glasses, the walker close by, a light at night; keep the call button and your phone within reach (NICE NG249).
More: Preventing falls after surgery · Walking frame: how to walk safely
## Preventing blood clots
- The team assesses your risk of blood clots; you may be given injections or tablets to prevent clots, compression stockings, or compression cuffs (inflatable sleeves that repeatedly squeeze and release your legs) (NICE NG89).
- Compression stockings are only given after your legs have been checked: they may be unsuitable if you have disease of the leg arteries, reduced feeling (for example, from diabetes) or damaged skin. Do not wear stockings that were not fitted for you (NICE NG89).
- If you are allowed to move your feet, regularly do the recommended ankle exercises (pull your feet up towards you, then point them away). They help keep your blood flowing, but they do not replace prescribed medicines or other measures to prevent blood clots.
- If you get new pain or swelling in your calf, tell the staff right away. Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain may be a sign of a dangerous complication — call the staff immediately. If this happens at home, call 101 (Magen David Adom).
More: Blood-clot injections at home: enoxaparin (Clexane, Crusia) · Leg swelling after surgery
## Breathing, drinking, eating, and bowels
- Unless the medical team has told you otherwise, breathe deeply and cough several times an hour — this helps your lungs. A nurse or physiotherapist will show you how to do it correctly.
- If you have no fluid restrictions, drink enough; eat a balanced diet (AAOS).
- After surgery, especially while taking opioid pain medicines, constipation is possible. Tell the nurse if you have trouble opening your bowels or have not had a bowel movement for several days. If you have severe abdominal pain, marked bloating, or vomiting, tell the nurse immediately.
More: Breathing exercises after surgery
## The wound, drain, and catheter
- Staff change the dressing. Do not touch the wound or remove the dressing yourself.
- Keep the dressing dry. The medical team will tell you when you can wash and how to protect the dressing from water.
- If you have a drain or a urinary catheter, do not pull on the tubes; tell the staff if a tube comes out, leaks, or causes pain.
- Tell the staff right away if the dressing becomes heavily soaked with blood or other fluid, or if redness, swelling, or pain around the wound gets worse. After spine surgery, it is especially important to report clear, watery fluid from the wound or a new severe headache (CUH NHS; Eur Spine J 2015).
- After spine surgery, new numbness in the area between your legs (perineum), increasing weakness in your legs, or problems controlling your bladder or bowels need immediate medical assessment (NICE NG127).
More: Changing the dressing on a surgical wound: when and with what · Preventing pressure ulcers
## Where you go after discharge: rehabilitation
After a hip or knee replacement, a hip fracture or an amputation, patients from our ward usually go to a rehabilitation hospital. The medical team will discuss with you whether further rehabilitation is needed and the appropriate setting — in hospital, as an outpatient or at home — based on your condition. Arrangements will be coordinated with your health fund. There, the rehabilitation team helps you get up, move around safely — walking or using a wheelchair if needed — and manage everyday activities. This helps prepare you for a safe return home. If you would prefer rehabilitation at home, tell the ward team in advance. This depends on your condition, your home situation and approval from your health fund. If home rehabilitation is arranged, a physiotherapist will come to your home.
## Before you go home (discharge), make sure you have in writing
- the doctor's discharge summary and prescriptions for the medicines you will need after discharge;
- which medicines to take, how much and for how long (including injections to prevent blood clots), and which of your own medicines to restart;
- what has changed in your medicines during your hospital stay and why;
- if you have been prescribed injections to prevent blood clots or other treatments at home — who will give them and how to do it correctly;
- wound care, when the stitches or staples will be removed, and when you can shower or bathe;
- how much weight you may put on your leg or arm, movement restrictions, exercises, and walking aids;
- what help you will need at home, whether it is safe for you to get up, walk, and use the toilet on your own, and whether you need physical therapy at home or further rehabilitation;
- if you will need equipment at home (for example, a walker or an oxygen concentrator) — how to get it before discharge and how to use it;
- the date of your follow-up visit and whom to call with questions;
- warning signs and when to call 101 (see the page “After surgery: warning signs”).
If anything is unclear, ask for it to be explained again. A good way to check yourself is to tell the nurse in your own words how to take your medicines and what to do at home. Also ask who will help you book your follow-up visit and any tests arranged at discharge.
## At home
- Remove rugs and cords, and make sure there is light at night; grab bars in the shower and by the toilet, a raised toilet seat, and a stable chair with armrests can help (AAOS).
- Increase your walking gradually and do the exercises your physical therapist gave you.
- Only start driving again after checking with your doctor and when you can drive safely. Do not drive if you feel sleepy or while taking opioid pain medicines that affect your attention and reactions (AAOS).
More: At home after hip replacement: aids and safe movements

## What this operation is
An amputation is the removal of part of a limb when it cannot be saved: for example, because of a severe infection or gangrene, often linked to diabetes or poor blood supply, or after a serious injury. The surgeon removes the damaged part, shapes the bones and covers them with muscle and skin to form a stump. The surgeon chooses the level of amputation after an assessment. The operation is done under general anaesthetic or under a spinal or epidural anaesthetic.
## Preparation
If the operation is planned, you will be assessed: your heart, blood vessels, lungs and your other leg are checked. The team may discuss rehabilitation, the possibility of a prosthesis and your home situation with you. Fasting, showering and medicines follow the general leaflet "Preparing for surgery". You can ask to meet someone who has had a similar operation.
## Medicines
Do not stop blood thinners or other regular medicines yourself — your doctor will tell you which ones to stop and when. Take painkillers as prescribed: some regularly, others only when needed. Before a physiotherapy session, check with your nurse in advance whether you need extra pain relief. Tell your nurse about any pain, including phantom pain.
## Drain and wound
Sometimes a drain is left in — the surgeon decides. In hospital, the medical staff change the dressing. Before discharge, check who will do your dressings afterwards and when. Do not remove the dressing yourself against the instructions you were given. Non-dissolvable stitches are removed by staff, usually after two to three weeks.
## After the operation
- Many people feel as if the amputated leg is still there (phantom sensations). In the first months it is easy to forget that the leg is gone, especially at night. Before getting up, stop and remember this; in hospital, press the call button.
- After a below-knee amputation, do not put a pillow under the stump and do not bend the bed under the knee — this helps keep the knee straight. In a chair, the stump rests on a special board.
- After an above-knee amputation, do not keep the hip bent for long periods, and do not keep a pillow under the stump or between the thighs all the time. Lie on your stomach or do hip-straightening exercises only if the rehabilitation team allows it.
- After removal of a toe or part of the foot, do not decide by yourself how much weight to put on the leg. The doctor may forbid weight-bearing for a while, allow partial weight-bearing, or allow walking with weight on the heel. Sometimes a special post-operative shoe is needed to take pressure off the wound: use it and your walking aids exactly as the physiotherapist showed you. Do not walk in an ordinary shoe without permission.
- Rehabilitation usually starts the next day: exercises in bed, then moving safely into a wheelchair. Hopping on one leg with crutches is not advised.
- Once the wound has healed, wash the stump daily with mild unscented soap and dry it carefully; check it every day.
- Protect your other leg, especially if you have diabetes: check your foot daily, wear comfortable shoes and see a foot specialist regularly.
- If the person spends a lot of time in bed, staff should use an appropriate device to take pressure off the heel of the remaining leg and help prevent a pressure ulcer.
- With diabetes, blood sugar after surgery can go up but can also drop, especially if the person is eating little and takes insulin or some diabetes tablets. If the patient skips a meal, tell the staff straight away. Also tell them immediately if the patient develops unusual weakness, sweating, shaking, dizziness, confusion or drowsiness. Do not change medicine doses yourself.
- In older people, sudden confusion (delirium) sometimes appears after a major operation: restlessness, disorientation — or the opposite, the person becomes quiet and drowsy. If you notice a sudden change, tell the nurse at once. More on the page “Confusion after surgery”.
- A prosthesis is not right for everyone: it depends on healing, muscle strength and general health. Without a prosthesis, you can still become independent with a wheelchair.
- Grief, anxiety and low mood after an amputation are a common and understandable reaction. Talk about your feelings with your family and the team; a psychologist can help.
- From our ward, after an amputation, people are usually referred to a rehabilitation hospital. The medical team will discuss with you whether further rehabilitation is needed and the appropriate setting — in hospital, as an outpatient or at home — based on your condition. Arrangements will be coordinated with your health fund.
## When to get urgent help
- The stump becomes red, hot or painful, the swelling increases, or there is discharge or pus from the wound — see a doctor the same day.
- If the wound edges become darker, the surrounding skin becomes noticeably colder, or the wound opens, tell the staff immediately.
- New pain or swelling in the calf of your other leg, especially on one side — get an urgent medical assessment.
- Painkillers are not helping — tell your nurse or doctor.
- Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain — call 101 straight away.
- If you have thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself, tell the medical staff immediately or get urgent professional help. If there is an immediate danger to life, call 101.
## The first hours after surgery: how to help without getting in the way
- A special time. The first hours and the first day after surgery are a time when the patient is watched especially closely. Most of the time everything goes calmly, but sometimes help is needed very quickly. That is why the way to the patient and to the medical equipment must always stay clear.
- The nurse checks. After returning to the ward, the nurse regularly checks blood pressure, pulse, breathing rate, blood oxygen level, temperature and level of consciousness, and also examines the surgical site. On our ward this is usually done on arrival from the operating theatre, then after one hour, after another hour, then after two hours, and then every four hours for the first day. Checks are done more often if needed. This is planned monitoring and does not necessarily mean that something is wrong. If enoxaparin (Clexane) is prescribed, the first postoperative injection on our ward is usually given about twelve hours after certain orthopaedic operations. The doctor decides the timing based on whether surgical bleeding is controlled, the type of operation, and any spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
- A clear path. Please leave a clear path to the bed on all sides. Do not put chairs or bags right next to the bed or in the way. The nurse and doctor must be able to reach the patient quickly, and the trolley with medicines and equipment must be able to get through.
- Fewer people is better. It is best if no more than one or two relatives are with the patient at the same time, if the ward rules allow it. When the staff come to the patient, please make room. If you are asked to leave the room, please do so. You can ask about the reason and ask questions later.
- Only with permission. After anaesthesia there can be nausea and vomiting, and a weak person may choke. After surgery, do not give the patient food or drink without the nurse’s permission. When the patient is awake enough, can swallow safely and there are no other restrictions, they may be allowed to drink and eat. Sometimes they start with a small amount of water and then move on to food. When and what is allowed is decided by the medical team.
- Rails and the button. Bed rails are raised when the staff decide, depending on the patient’s condition. They are not suitable for everyone and do not replace help with getting up. Do not raise or lower the rails yourself. The call button should be within reach. Get up only with the medical team’s permission, and the first time with the staff’s help.
- If something is wrong. If the patient feels sick, finds it hard to breathe, becomes unusually drowsy or hard to wake, suddenly becomes confused or very restless, the pain gets much worse, or the dressing quickly soaks with blood, call the nurse at once. Press the call button, and if the situation is serious, call the staff loudly. Do not give food, drink or medicines yourself, and do not try to lift the patient.
## Preventing infection in hospital: what patients and families can do
- Clean hands. Clean your hands with hand sanitiser or soap and water when entering and leaving the room, and before and after touching the patient. Wash your hands with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Hand sanitiser is usually by the room entrance or next to the bed. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.
- Avoid other patients' rooms. Do not walk around other rooms and wards of the hospital without need. Relatives with fever, cough, runny nose, vomiting or diarrhoea should not visit until they have recovered.
- Resistant bacteria. Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. If these bacteria are found in a patient, staff may introduce additional precautions, such as isolation, gowns and gloves, or special visiting rules. The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.
- Higher risk. Take extra care to prevent infection with open fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, external fixators (pins or wires pass through the skin into the bone), diabetes, and after complex or prolonged operations. The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.
- Tell the nurse straight away if: there is fever or chills; redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge from the wound; redness or discharge around the external fixator pin or wire sites, or around the IV catheter site.

## What this fracture is
The ankle joint is formed by the lower ends of the shinbone (tibia) and the thinner leg bone (fibula), and by the talus bone of the foot. The bony parts on the inner and outer sides and at the back of the ankle are called malleoli: the lateral malleolus (outer side, end of the fibula), the medial malleolus (inner side, end of the tibia), and the posterior malleolus (back edge of the tibia) (AAOS).Right ankle. Left: front view; right: view from the outer side. 1 — shinbone (tibia); 2 — fibula; 3 — medial malleolus (part of the tibia); 4 — lateral malleolus (lower end of the fibula); 5 — talus; 6 — syndesmosis ligaments between the tibia and fibula; 7 — posterior malleolus (back edge of the tibia). Light blue line — joint cartilage. Simplified drawing
- A fracture of the lateral malleolus alone is often stable.
- In a bimalleolar fracture, the lateral and medial malleoli are broken; in a trimalleolar fracture, all three are broken. These fractures are often unstable and often occur together with a dislocation (AAOS).
- Sometimes the ligaments that hold the tibia and fibula together (the syndesmosis) are also injured.
- Signs: severe pain, swelling, bruising, being unable to put weight on the injured foot, and sometimes a visible deformity.
## When surgery is needed
- The surgeon decides based on the examination and X-rays. Surgery is usually offered when the bone fragments are out of place or the joint is unstable: often in bimalleolar and trimalleolar fractures and in an unstable syndesmosis injury (AAOS; BOAST).
- A stable fracture that is not out of place is often treated without surgery — with a cast or a special walking boot.
- If there is a wound at the fracture site, even a small one, the fracture may be open. The bone does not have to be visible from the outside. This kind of fracture needs medical care right away (NICE NG37; AAOS).
## Before surgery
- The leg is in a splint or cast and is kept raised above the level of the heart: this reduces swelling (AAOS).
- If there is a lot of swelling, the final operation is sometimes delayed to lower the risk of problems with the skin and the surgical wound. However, if the joint is dislocated or badly deformed, it may need to be put back in place urgently and held temporarily, without waiting for the swelling to go down. The surgeon decides when the final operation will be done (BOAST).
- Tell the staff if the pain under the splint gets worse, or if you have numbness or tingling, or your foot becomes cold or pale.
- Smoking, diabetes, and older age increase the risk of complications after surgery (AAOS). If you smoke, talk with the team about how to stop, at least during your treatment.
## How the operation is done
- The surgeon puts the bone fragments back in place and holds them with a plate and screws or with screws alone; sometimes a rod is used. The syndesmosis is held with a screw or with a special cord with buttons (AAOS).
- After surgery, the leg is usually held in a splint, cast, or boot.
## After surgery on the ward
- Keep your leg raised and move your toes.
- How much weight you may put on your leg depends on the type of fracture, how firm the fixation is, and the condition of the soft tissues. Some patients are allowed to put weight on the leg early; others must avoid putting any weight on the injured leg for some time. Do not put more weight on your injured leg than your surgeon allows: follow the surgeon's written instructions. The physical therapist will teach you to walk safely with crutches or a walker (BOAST; WAX, The Lancet 2024).
- When you have a fracture and cannot move as usual, the medical team assesses your risk of blood clots and of bleeding. If needed, the doctor prescribes a medicine to prevent blood clots. The choice of medicine and how long you take it are decided individually. If a medicine has been prescribed, do not stop taking it on your own (NICE NG89).
- Tell the staff right away about: pain that does not get better with pain medicine; new numbness or tingling, or a pale or cold foot; a dressing that is soaked through; pain or swelling in the calf; sudden shortness of breath or chest pain.
## At home and recovery
- Early healing of the bone usually takes at least 6 weeks, and complete healing 10–12 weeks; ligaments and tendons may need longer (AAOS).
- Follow-up X-rays are done at the times your doctor sets.
- Keep the cast and dressing dry unless the medical team has told you otherwise. Do not put anything inside the cast, and do not try to cut or remove it yourself. If the cast gets wet, breaks, rubs badly, or there is a bad smell or discharge from under it, contact your treating team or go to the emergency department without delay (NHS).
- Swelling and stiffness in the ankle may last for several months (AAOS).
- Most people return to their usual activities 3–4 months after the injury; full recovery after some fractures can take up to 2 years (AAOS).
- You may return to driving once your doctor agrees, when you can control the car safely and make an emergency stop without pain or limitation. Do not drive if a splint, cast, or boot on your right leg, or medicines that make you sleepy, stop you from driving safely. For a fracture of the right ankle, AAOS gives an approximate time of 9–12 weeks, but this is not a required minimum for everyone.
- Screws and plates often stay in the bone. Whether they need to be removed is decided by the surgeon.
## When to get urgent help
- If at home you suddenly become short of breath or have chest pain — call 101.
- If your foot becomes cold, pale, or blue, you have marked numbness, the pain suddenly gets much worse and does not ease with your prescribed pain medicine, or the cast suddenly feels very tight — go to the emergency department (ER) right away. Do not wait until the next day.
- If you have a temperature of 38 °C or higher, redness or discharge from the wound, or new pain or swelling in the calf, you need a medical check on the same day (see the page "After surgery: warning signs").

## Why breathing exercises matter
After anaesthesia, and because of pain and lying in bed for a long time, people often breathe shallowly and cough little. Some parts of the lungs do not open up as well, and phlegm is harder to clear from the airways. This raises the risk of lung complications, including infections. Deep breathing, coughing and moving early help prevent this.
## How to do them
- Sit upright: in a chair, or in bed with the head raised, if this is allowed. Relax your shoulders.
- Breathe in slowly and deeply, preferably through your nose. Hold your breath for about 3 seconds. Breathe out slowly through your mouth.
- Take 3–5 of these breaths, then rest: more in a row can make you dizzy.
- Then try to cough if there is phlegm. If coughing hurts, first take a deep breath in, then breathe out forcefully through an open mouth, as if steaming up a mirror. This helps move the phlegm so it is easier to cough up. Coughing does not usually damage stitches.
- Repeat several times an hour while you are awake, or as your physiotherapist advises.
- If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe calmly.
## What else helps
- Pain relief: if pain makes it hard to breathe deeply and cough, tell the nurse.
- Moving: sit up and walk as soon as the team allows. The first time you get up, only with the staff.
- Do not slide down the bed: a half-sitting position makes breathing easier.
## When to tell staff at once
- Breathlessness, chest pain, a fever, a cough with phlegm, or feeling worse — tell the nurse straight away.
- At home: sudden breathlessness or chest pain — call 101. A fever or a cough with phlegm — see a doctor.

## What it is
- Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the deep layers of the skin and the tissue under it. It is most often on the leg, but it can appear anywhere on the body.
- This is not cosmetic “cellulite” (orange-peel skin) — it is something completely different.
- Ordinary cellulitis without pus is most often caused by streptococci. Staphylococcus aureus is less common, mainly when there is an open wound or a penetrating skin injury.
- Bacteria get in through damaged skin: a small cut, a crack, a bite, athlete's foot between the toes, an ulcer or a surgical incision.
- Erysipelas is a similar infection of the more superficial layers of the skin; it is treated in a similar way.
- Cellulitis usually does not pass from person to person through ordinary everyday contact.
## What it looks like
- Red skin that may spread.
- Swelling, skin that feels hot to the touch, and tenderness.
- Sometimes blisters on the skin.
- There may be fever, chills and weakness.
- Usually only one leg is affected. Redness on both legs at once is more often due to other causes (for example, blood pooling in the veins or a skin reaction) — the doctor decides.
## Who is at higher risk
- Leg swelling, poor lymph drainage (lymphoedema), varicose veins.
- Diabetes, a weakened immune system.
- Excess weight.
- Cracked skin, athlete's foot, eczema, wounds and ulcers.
- Having had cellulitis before: it can come back.
## How we treat it on the ward
- The doctor examines the skin and rules out other causes of redness and swelling, such as blood pooling in the veins, a skin reaction or a deep vein thrombosis. In ordinary cellulitis, cultures are usually not needed. Blood tests, a wound culture and an ultrasound scan are ordered when needed, depending on the patient's condition.
- The main treatment is antibiotics. The doctor prescribes them as tablets or into a vein, depending on how severe the infection is.
- If a collection of pus (an abscess) has formed under the skin, it may need to be opened and drained. Antibiotics alone are sometimes not enough.
- The edge of the redness is often outlined with a marker pen. This makes it easier to see whether the infection is shrinking or spreading. Do not wipe this line off.
- The leg is kept raised: this reduces swelling and pain and helps healing.
- The first signs of improvement usually appear within two to three days of starting treatment. In the first 24–48 hours the redness sometimes spreads a little, but rapid worsening needs a doctor's assessment straight away. Even when treatment works, redness and swelling of the skin can last longer, sometimes several weeks.
- Take the antibiotic for the whole time the doctor prescribed, even if you feel better. Do not stop the treatment on your own.
- The “entry point” of the infection is treated too: athlete's foot between the toes, cracks, dry skin and wounds.
## Tell staff straight away, and at home seek urgent help (if severely unwell, call 101), if
- The redness spreads quickly beyond the line drawn with the marker.
- Severe pain that seems out of proportion to how the skin looks.
- The skin darkens or turns bluish or black, blisters with dark fluid appear, or the skin goes numb.
- High fever, chills, severe weakness, confusion, fast breathing or a fast heartbeat, dizziness.
- If a rash or itching appears after starting the antibiotic, tell the medical staff straight away. If you have sudden swelling of the face, lips or tongue, difficulty breathing, severe dizziness or you pass out, call staff immediately; at home, call an ambulance on 101.
- If redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge appear around the surgical wound, especially after a joint replacement, tell the surgeon straight away. This may be a surgical site infection, which needs its own assessment and treatment.
- There is no improvement after 2–3 days of treatment.
## How to lower the risk of it coming back
- Check your legs and feet every day, especially if you have diabetes.
- Moisturise dry skin, and treat athlete's foot and cracks.
- Reduce leg swelling: raise your legs when resting; use compression stockings only if a doctor recommends them.
- Wash your hands and look after small cuts; keep any wound clean.
- If cellulitis keeps coming back, the doctor may discuss special preventive treatment.
- At the first signs of it coming back, see a doctor — do not wait.
## Preventing infection in hospital: what patients and families can do
- Clean hands. Clean your hands with hand sanitiser or soap and water when entering and leaving the room, and before and after touching the patient. Wash your hands with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Hand sanitiser is usually by the room entrance or next to the bed. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.
- Avoid other patients' rooms. Do not walk around other rooms and wards of the hospital without need. Relatives with fever, cough, runny nose, vomiting or diarrhoea should not visit until they have recovered.
- Resistant bacteria. Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. If these bacteria are found in a patient, staff may introduce additional precautions, such as isolation, gowns and gloves, or special visiting rules. The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.
- Higher risk. Take extra care to prevent infection with open fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, external fixators (pins or wires pass through the skin into the bone), diabetes, and after complex or prolonged operations. The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.
- Tell the nurse straight away if: there is fever or chills; redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge from the wound; redness or discharge around the external fixator pin or wire sites, or around the IV catheter site.

## What CPE and VRE are
- CPE and VRE are groups of bacteria that are resistant to certain antibiotics.
- CPE are bacteria, such as E. coli or Klebsiella, that produce enzymes able to break down antibiotics of the carbapenem group.
- VRE are enterococci that are resistant to the antibiotic vancomycin.
- They can live in the gut without causing illness. This is called carriage. A person can feel completely well, but the bacteria can be passed on to other patients.
- In hospital they spread mainly by contact: through contaminated hands, medical equipment and surrounding objects. If these bacteria cause an infection, finding an effective antibiotic can be harder.
## How the swab is taken
- A nurse or another trained staff member gently inserts a sterile swab a short way into the anus to take a sample from the rectum.
- It takes only a short time. It usually does not hurt, but you may feel brief discomfort.
- For a reliable test, a small amount of stool should be on the swab. In some cases a stool sample is used instead.
## Why on admission and during the hospital stay
- In Israel there are rules for finding carriers of resistant bacteria in hospitals.
- For CPE, screening is especially important after a transfer from another healthcare facility, after a recent hospital stay, after contact with a known carrier, and in high-risk wards.
- For VRE, the hospital infection control team decides which groups of patients are tested on admission and when repeat tests are needed.
- On our ward the swab is usually taken on admission and repeated after about fourteen days if the patient is still in hospital. This is a local routine, not a uniform requirement for all hospitals in Israel.
- A repeat swab helps detect carriage that was not found before. It does not necessarily mean the doctors suspect an infection.
## If the result is positive
- A positive swab does not necessarily mean illness. If the bacterium is found but is not causing an infection, this is carriage, and on its own it usually does not need antibiotics.
- In hospital, staff use contact isolation so the bacteria do not pass to other patients: depending on the bacterium, a separate room or a dedicated area, separate equipment, gowns and gloves.
- Isolation is not a punishment. The treatment you need continues, with safety measures in place.
- Information about carriage is kept in your medical record: at future hospital stays or transfers it helps staff choose precautions in advance.
- A negative repeat swab on its own does not mean the carriage is gone and isolation can be stopped.
## When isolation is stopped
- Carriage sometimes lasts for months.
- The decision to stop isolation is made by the hospital infection control team according to special rules; several negative tests at set intervals may be needed.
- One negative swab is not a reason to decide for yourself that the carriage is over. Do not stop isolation measures without instructions from staff.
## What you and your family can do
- Wash or sanitise your hands: after using the toilet, before eating, and when entering and leaving the room.
- Always sanitise your hands before and after caring for a wound, a catheter or another medical device. If you may touch blood, stool or other body fluids, wear gloves as staff instruct.
- Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not share personal items.
- At home, ordinary hygiene is usually enough; healthy family members generally do not need special isolation.
- Whenever you next go to a hospital, clinic or rehabilitation centre, always tell them about any CPE or VRE carriage found in the past.

## What delirium is
Delirium is a sudden disturbance of awareness and attention ("acute confusion"). It develops over hours or days, it can change during the day and often gets worse in the evening and at night. Delirium is common in older people after surgery, especially after a broken hip, and it is usually temporary.
## Signs
- Mixes up time and place, has trouble understanding speech and concentrating.
- Sees or hears things that are not there; restless or agitated.
- "Quiet" delirium: unusual sleepiness and slowness, responds little — it is easy to miss.
## Who is more at risk and why
The risk is higher in older people, in dementia or memory problems, in serious illness, and after a broken hip and other major operations. Causes and contributing factors include infection, pain, dehydration, some medicines, constipation or urine retention, poor sleep, unfamiliar surroundings, and poor eyesight or hearing. They often occur together.
## What families can do
- If the person suddenly behaves differently, tell the staff straight away. What you notice matters a lot.
- Speak calmly, in short simple sentences; remind them where they are, what day it is and why they are in hospital; do not argue.
- Bring glasses, hearing aids, dentures, familiar objects and photos.
- Daytime: light, a clock and calendar in view; night: quiet and sleep. Help them drink and eat, if this is allowed.
## What the team does
The team looks for the cause and treats it: infection, pain, dehydration, medicines, constipation or urine retention. If delirium causes severe distress or a risk of harm to the person or others, and calming without medication has not helped, the doctor may prescribe medicine at the lowest appropriate dose for a short time.
## Afterwards
Improvement usually begins within a few days, but recovery can take weeks or months. The person may not remember this period well. If confusion persists after discharge, contact a doctor. If confusion suddenly appears or rapidly worsens at home, call 101 immediately.

## Why it is dangerous
- Over time, high blood sugar damages the nerves: the foot feels pain, heat and tight shoes less well. A callus, a burn or a small stone in the shoe can go unnoticed.
- Blood flow to the legs is often reduced, so wounds heal slowly.
- Infection can spread quickly deep into the foot, down to the bone. Without treatment this can end in amputation.
## How we treat it on the ward
- The doctor examines the wound and assesses its depth, signs of infection, feeling in the foot and blood flow in both legs. Blood tests are done when needed.
- If a bone infection is suspected, an X-ray is usually done first; if the diagnosis is still unclear, an MRI may be needed. Blood flow is checked with a vascular ultrasound and by measuring the pressure in the arteries of the leg or toes.
- Not every wound needs a culture. If there are signs of infection, the doctor may take a tissue sample from deep in the wound after cleaning it, to identify the bacteria and choose an antibiotic.
- Antibiotics are given when there is an infection. If the wound is not infected, they do not speed up healing. In a severe infection, treatment starts straight away without waiting for the culture result.
- Wound cleaning: when needed, a specialist removes dead tissue. If blood flow is very poor, the approach is chosen according to the state of the blood vessels.
- Taking pressure off the foot: for the wound to heal, pressure on it must be reduced — with a special brace, a cast, a shoe or mobility aids. Sometimes you must not stand on the foot at all; sometimes it is allowed only in a special device. The medical team will explain the rules.
- If blood flow is reduced, a vascular specialist must assess it; sometimes a procedure to restore blood flow is needed. Blood sugar is also controlled, and infection and wound-care specialists are involved when needed.
## What the patient and family can do
- Do not stand on the affected foot if you have been told not to. Even a few barefoot steps can damage the wound.
- Do not remove, change or wet the dressing unless the medical staff tell you to. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Good nutrition with enough calories and protein is important for healing. If the patient is eating little, losing weight or has kidney disease, ask the doctor or dietitian for individual advice.
- If the patient spends a lot of time in bed, both heels — especially the heel of the other leg — are protected from pressure with a special pillow or device chosen by the staff. Do not put anything under the leg yourself if it interferes with the prescribed leg position.
- Smoking reduces blood flow — talk to your doctor about stopping smoking.
## When to tell the staff at once
- Fever, chills, sudden severe weakness, unusual drowsiness or confusion; redness or swelling spreading quickly; pus, discharge, a bad smell or darkening skin — tell the staff immediately.
- With diabetes, even a severe infection sometimes causes no strong pain and no high fever.
- Blackening of the toes or part of the foot, a sudden very cold foot, rapidly spreading inflammation or feeling very unwell — this needs emergency medical assessment.
- If the patient takes insulin or tablets that can lower blood sugar, report a skipped meal, sweating, shaking, sudden weakness or confusion.
## A hot, swollen foot without a wound
- With diabetes and nerve damage, the foot can suddenly become red, hot and swollen — even without a wound and without strong pain. Sometimes this is damage to the bones and joints called Charcot foot.
- Do not wait for pain: an urgent specialist examination is needed. Until then, do not put weight on that foot.
## Before discharge, ask
- Who will change the dressing at home, and how.
- How much weight you may put on the affected foot, and which offloading device to use.
- When and where the next wound check will be.
- How to check blood sugar and take your medicines.
- Where to turn if the wound gets worse. If the patient cannot check their feet or follow the instructions alone, arrange help from family or home-care staff in advance.
## At home: protect both feet
- Check your feet every day, including between the toes — with a mirror or with help from family.

- Dry the skin gently, especially between the toes. Moisturise dry areas, but do not put cream between the toes or on an open wound unless told to.
- Do not walk barefoot. Check inside your shoes with your hand before putting them on. Wear socks without tight elastic.
- Cut toenails straight across, without cutting into the corners. If nails are thick, hard to see or unsafe to cut yourself, see a foot-care specialist. Do not cut corns or calluses yourself and do not use corn plasters or liquids.

## The first 48 hours
- On our ward, the first planned dressing change is usually done about 48 hours after surgery. This is our ward's routine, not a single rule for all hospitals.
- This is consistent with section 6.3.7 of the Israeli Ministry of Health inspection checklist: the first dressing change is carried out after 24–48 hours according to the local protocol. The CDC's 1999 guideline also advised protecting a closed surgical incision with a sterile dressing for the first 24–48 hours. That guideline has since been superseded.
- The dressing is changed earlier if it is soaked with blood or fluid, has come loose or is dirty. A nurse does this.
- Some special dressings are left on longer, as the surgeon decides.
## How the dressing is changed
- The nurse cleans her hands and changes the dressing using aseptic technique, with the right gloves and materials so the wound does not get contaminated. She checks the wound for redness, swelling, discharge and edges coming apart.
- In the first 48 hours the wound is cleaned with sterile saline if needed.
- After 48 hours, a clean, dry, well-closed wound usually does not need any solution: the nurse checks it and puts on a clean dressing if needed. If there is discharge or crusting, the wound may be gently cleaned with saline.
- If a dressing is needed after the examination, the nurse applies a new sterile dressing.
- Do not touch the wound with your hands, and do not remove or adjust the dressing yourself.
## Why not alcohol on the wound
- Alcohol disinfects intact skin well (for example, before an injection or surgery), but it is not put on an open wound or a fresh incision: it stings badly, damages the living cells in the wound and can slow healing.
## Iodine antiseptic (“Polydine”)
- For an ordinary clean surgical wound that is healing with its edges together and without problems, international guidance advises against routinely applying antiseptics just to prevent infection.
- On our ward, povidone-iodine (for example, Polydine 10% solution without alcohol) may be used when there is a specific medical reason, as the doctor or nurse decides, following the approved protocol. It is not a required step at every dressing change.
- The solution can irritate the skin. If you notice redness, itching, a rash or burning under the dressing, tell staff.
- Be sure to tell the doctor or nurse if you have had a reaction to an iodine product, have a thyroid or kidney condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or take lithium.
- The different forms of the product (solution, alcohol-based tincture, soap) are not interchangeable. The alcohol-based tincture is not put on a wound.
## Showering
- You can often shower after 48 hours if the doctor or nurse allows it. Do not soak the wound: no bath, swimming pool or sea until it has healed.
- When and how to wash depends on the operation and the type of dressing (some dressings are waterproof). Ask the staff.
## What the research shows
- A Cochrane review (2015) found no clear difference in wound infection rates whether the dressing was removed within the first 48 hours or later. The certainty of the evidence is very low, so the ward sets the exact timing for each operation.
- A Cochrane review (2022) found insufficient reliable evidence to determine whether infection risk differs when wounds are cleaned with drinking water or saline. The review included different types of wounds. This does not change the recommendation to use sterile saline if a surgical wound needs cleaning during the first 48 hours.
- NICE and CDC guidance advise against putting antiseptics or antibiotics on a clean stitched wound just to prevent infection.
## At home
- Dressing changes — as written in your discharge letter: who does them, how often and with what.
- Do not put creams, ointments, alcohol or iodine on the wound unless they were prescribed.
- Seek help urgently (if severely unwell, call 101): redness and swelling around the wound are increasing, pus or a bad smell, the wound has opened, fever, or pain getting worse.

## The membrane around the nerves
- The spinal cord and nerve roots are surrounded by a tough membrane. Inside it is a clear fluid that bathes the nerves.
## What happened
- Sometimes this membrane tears during spine surgery. This is a known complication, especially in repeat operations and when the canal is severely narrowed. Usually the surgeon stitches or seals the tear straight away.
## What it can cause
- If fluid keeps leaking through the tear, the pressure around the brain drops. This can cause a particular kind of headache. In most cases, once repaired, everything heals without lasting effects.
## The surgeon decides
- After a tear, the routine varies. Some patients may get up at the usual time; others are told to lie flat for a while. Once the tear is closed, studies have not shown a convincing benefit of prolonged bed rest over getting up early. But it depends on the situation, so the surgeon decides. Follow your surgeon's instructions.
## If you need to stay in bed
- While lying down, move your feet, do breathing exercises and drink if you are allowed. If clot prevention has been prescribed, do not miss it. Sit or stand up only when allowed, and only with the staff.
## Headache
- Tell the staff if you get a headache that gets worse when sitting or standing and eases when lying down. It may come with nausea, neck pain or ringing in the ears. This can be a sign of a fluid leak.
## Wound and drain
- Tell the staff straight away if your dressing gets wet with clear, watery fluid, if such fluid appears in the drain container, or if a soft swelling appears under the wound. Clear discharge does not always mean a leak, but it must be checked.
## No straining
- Try not to strain. If you are constipated, say so; you may be given a laxative. Do not lift anything heavy until the surgeon allows it.
## Urgent
- Tell the medical team immediately if the headache is very severe, or you develop a fever, neck pain and stiffness, sensitivity to light, or confusion. Also report immediately any new weakness or numbness in the legs, numbness around the genitals or anus, new difficulty passing urine, or loss of bladder or bowel control.
## At home
- If these signs appear at home, go to the emergency department immediately. If there is confusion, new marked weakness, loss of bladder or bowel control, or another serious condition, call one zero one. Do not drive yourself.

## Why you need the injections
After an operation on the leg, especially after a hip or knee replacement and after fractures, the risk of blood clots in the leg veins goes up. So the doctor may prescribe enoxaparin injections — for example Clexane or Crusia — after you go home too. Give them for as many days as your discharge letter says: do not miss doses and do not stop early, otherwise the protection against clots is reduced.
- Inject yourself only if a nurse or doctor has shown you how to do it. If you cannot inject yourself, arrange in advance who will help: a relative or a community nurse.
- Before your first injection at home, read the instructions for the exact syringe you were given. The technique below is for Crusia. Clexane and other prefilled syringes may have different safety mechanisms. If you were given a medicine or syringe that looks different, ask the nurse or pharmacist how to use it. Do not change the medicine or the dose yourself.
- If your kidneys do not work well, or you have ever had a serious reaction to heparin with a drop in platelets, make sure your doctor knows. With some kidney conditions the enoxaparin dose must be changed, and after certain reactions to heparin the medicine may not be allowed. Never change the dose yourself.
## Before the injection
- Before every injection, check the name of the medicine, the prescribed dose, the expiry date and that the syringe is undamaged. The solution should be clear. If the syringe is damaged, out of date or the solution is cloudy, do not use it. This helps avoid a dose error or giving a medicine that is not fit for use.
- Wash your hands with soap and water and dry them well — this lowers the risk of infection at the injection site.
- Sit or lie down comfortably.
- Choose a spot on the right or left side of your belly, at least 5 cm from the belly button.
- Do not inject into scars, bruises, or areas that are sore, red or swollen. If redness, pain, swelling or discharge from the skin remains after a previous injection, tell the nurse or doctor. This may be a sign of a local complication.
- Change sides each time: one day right, the next day left. This means fewer bruises and lumps under the skin.
- Wipe the skin with an alcohol wipe or with soap and water and let it dry.
## How to give the injection
- Right or left side of the belly, at least 5 cm from the belly button. Change sides each time.
- Gently pinch a fold of belly skin between your thumb and index finger and hold it for the whole injection.
- Insert the whole length of the needle into the fold at a right angle and press the plunger slowly until all the medicine has gone in.
- Pull the needle straight out, without changing direction.Diagram, not to scale. The syringe safety device works as described in the instructions for your syringe (see below).
- Carefully pull off the needle cap. Do not let the needle touch anything — it must stay sterile.
- If the prefilled syringe contains exactly your prescribed dose, do not push out the air bubble and do not press the plunger before the injection — otherwise you may lose some of the medicine. If your prescribed dose is smaller than the contents of the syringe, change the amount yourself only after special training by a nurse or doctor.
- Hold the syringe in the hand you write with. With your other hand, gently pinch a fold of belly skin between your thumb and index finger, and hold the fold for the whole injection.
- Insert the whole length of the needle into the skin fold at a right angle.
- Press the plunger slowly until all the medicine has gone in.
- After the whole dose is in, keep your finger on the plunger and pull the needle straight out, without changing direction. Point the needle away from yourself and others. Only after the needle is out, press the plunger firmly to activate the Crusia safety system. The safety sleeve covers the needle, usually with a click. Then let go of the skin fold. This helps prevent an accidental needle-stick from the used needle.
- Do not rub or scratch the injection site: this makes bruising more likely.
## After the injection
- Put the used syringe straight into a special strong container for sharp medical waste. Do not put the cap back on, do not reuse the syringe and do not throw it in the ordinary rubbish bin. This prevents accidental needle-sticks. Ask your clinic or pharmacy in advance where to get a container and where to return it when it is full.
- Store unused syringes below 25°C, do not freeze them, and keep them away from children. Do not leave the medicine in a hot car or in direct sunlight. Wrong storage can affect the quality of the medicine.
- A small bruise or a small lump at the injection site is common and usually goes away by itself.
- Mark each injection you give on a calendar — this lowers the risk of forgetting or repeating a dose.
## If you missed an injection or made a dose error
- If you forgot a Crusia injection, the manufacturer’s instructions say to give the missed dose as soon as you remember. Do not give a double dose on the same day to make up for it: this increases the risk of bleeding. If it is nearly time for the next injection, or you are not sure whether you gave the medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist what to do next.
- If you accidentally gave too much or too little, contact your doctor, nurse or pharmacist straight away, even if you feel fine. Do not try to make up for the error with another injection: too high a dose increases the risk of bleeding.
## When to get help
- Enoxaparin reduces blood clotting and increases the risk of bleeding. If you notice blood in your urine, repeated nosebleeds, or unusually large bruises, seek medical help the same day. If your stools are black and tarry, go to the emergency department immediately: this may be a sign of bleeding in the digestive tract.
- If you vomit blood, have heavy bleeding that cannot be stopped, a sudden very severe headache, loss of consciousness or sudden weakness on one side of the body — call 101 immediately. These may be signs of serious bleeding, a stroke, or another medical emergency.
- If you have had spinal or epidural anesthesia, a lumbar puncture, or spine surgery, and develop new severe pain in the middle of your back, numbness or increasing weakness in your legs, or loss of bladder or bowel control, go to the emergency department immediately. If your condition worsens rapidly, call 101.
- If you fall and hit your head during treatment, go to the emergency department immediately, even if you feel well for now. Hidden bleeding is possible while you take this medicine.
- If after an injection your lips, tongue or throat swell or you have difficulty breathing — call 101 immediately: this may be a severe allergic reaction.
- Do not add aspirin, ibuprofen or other anti-inflammatory medicines on your own: together with enoxaparin they increase the risk of bleeding. If your doctor has already prescribed aspirin, do not stop it yourself.
- New pain, swelling or redness in the calf — get medical help the same day: it may be a blood clot.
- Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain — call 101 straight away.

## Why people fall after surgery
After surgery the risk of falling is higher: weakness, dizziness, painkillers and sleeping tablets, an unfamiliar room, a drip and tubes, and trips to the toilet at night. A fall can cause a new injury, so it is important to prevent it.
## In hospital
- Keep the call button within reach. The first time, and whenever you have been told to get up only with help, press the button and wait for the staff.
- Get up slowly: first sit on the edge of the bed. If you feel dizzy, lie down again and call the staff. Stand up only once the dizziness has passed, and with staff help if you have been told you need it.
- Walk with the walking aid chosen for you; keep the walking frame or crutches next to the bed. Do not lean on the bedside cabinet, a wheeled table or the drip stand.
- Closed shoes that fit well with non-slip soles; glasses and hearing aid if you need them; a light at night.
## At home
- Clear rugs, cables and clutter out of the way. In the bathroom: a non-slip mat and grab rails. At night: a light on the way to the toilet. Keep your phone with you.
- If medicines make you sleepy or dizzy, talk to your doctor or pharmacist. Do not stop them yourself.
- Have your eyesight and hearing checked regularly.
- When your doctor allows it, do strength and balance exercises from your physiotherapist's programme. Drink enough, unless you have a fluid restriction.
## If you fall
- In hospital: do not get up on your own. Press the call button or call the staff.
- At home: if there is severe pain (especially in the operated joint), a possible injury to the head, neck, back or hip, or you cannot get up, do not try to get up on your own and call 101 immediately.
- Tell your doctor about any fall.

## Spinal fusion and discectomy: what you need to know
## What these operations are
Discectomy is removal of part of an intervertebral disc (a herniation) that presses on a nerve root. It is often done through a small incision (microdiscectomy) or with an endoscope (AAOS).
Spinal fusion is an operation in which two or more vertebrae are joined together so that there is no movement between them. A bone graft is used for this (your own bone, donor bone, or a synthetic bone-graft substitute), and often also screws, rods, or plates that hold the vertebrae while the bone heals together (AAOS).Left: a herniated disc presses on the nerves in the spinal canal and is removed (discectomy). Right: spinal fusion — the vertebrae are joined with screws and a rod, with a cage containing bone graft between the vertebral bodies. The screws pass through the pedicles of the vertebrae, beside the spinal canal; in this side view they appear to overlap the canal. Simplified drawing
## When they are done
- Most people with a herniated disc get better without surgery within several weeks or months (AAOS). Surgery (decompression) is considered for sciatica when treatment without surgery has not helped and the images match the symptoms (NICE NG59, 1.3.8).
- New inability to urinate or trouble passing urine, loss of control over urine or stool, numbness around the genitals, between the legs, or around the anus (saddle area), and rapidly worsening leg weakness can point to compression of nerve structures. This is an emergency that needs immediate evaluation in a hospital. If you get worse quickly, call 101. (Sources: AAOS; Guy's and St Thomas' NHS)
- Spinal fusion is done for some spine conditions when a segment of the spine needs to be stabilized: for example, instability, spondylolisthesis, certain deformities, or fractures. For non-specific low back pain without such a surgical indication, NICE does not recommend spinal fusion outside a randomized trial. The surgeon decides after your examination whether the operation is needed (AAOS; NICE NG59, 1.3.9).
## Preparation
See the leaflet "Preparing for Surgery": fasting, medicines (especially blood thinners - only as your doctor tells you), showering. Smoking raises the risk that the bones will not fuse after spinal fusion (AAOS) - talk with your doctor about quitting smoking.
## After surgery
- After surgery, the staff will help you start moving and walking as soon as it is safe, given your condition and the type of operation. The surgeon and the rehabilitation team decide the timing, how much weight and activity are allowed, whether you need a brace, and any restrictions (NICE NG89; AAOS).
- After spine surgery, prevention of blood clots is chosen individually, taking into account the risk of clots forming and of bleeding after surgery. Mechanical prevention methods may be used. Your doctor decides whether and when to start medicines against clots. Do not start such medicines on your own (NICE NG89).
- Wound care - follow the instructions you get at discharge.
## Possible complications
- Infection of the surgical wound is a possible complication. Preventive antibiotics are given before spinal fusion surgery when implants are placed. For other spine operations without implants, the need for preventive antibiotics depends on the type of procedure and the hospital protocol (NICE NG125).
- Blood clots in the leg veins.
- Possible complications also include injury to nerve structures, bleeding, and leakage of spinal fluid. These complications do not happen to every patient (CUH; NHS).
- After spinal fusion: the bones may not fuse (pseudarthrosis), more often in smokers, with diabetes, and in older age; sometimes a repeat operation is needed (AAOS).
- After discectomy, part of the intervertebral disc stays in place, so the herniation may form again later and symptoms may return (UCLH).
## When to get urgent help
- If you have new or rapidly worsening leg weakness, numbness around the genitals, between the legs, or around the anus (saddle area), difficulty starting or passing urine, loss of the usual feeling that you need to urinate, inability to urinate, or new loss of bladder or bowel control, go to the emergency department (ER) immediately. Do not drive yourself. If you cannot get there safely, call 101 (Magen David Adom). If you have sudden shortness of breath or chest pain, call 101 immediately.
- Go to the emergency department (ER) urgently if clear, watery fluid leaks from your surgical wound, even without other symptoms, or if you develop a new headache that gets worse when sitting or standing and improves when lying down. These may be signs of a spinal fluid leak.
- Fever, redness, or discharge from the wound; pain and swelling in the leg - see a doctor the same day (see the leaflet "After surgery: warning signs").
## The first hours after surgery: how to help without getting in the way
- A special time. The first hours and the first day after surgery are a time when the patient is watched especially closely. Most of the time everything goes calmly, but sometimes help is needed very quickly. That is why the way to the patient and to the medical equipment must always stay clear.
- The nurse checks. After returning to the ward, the nurse regularly checks blood pressure, pulse, breathing rate, blood oxygen level, temperature and level of consciousness, and also examines the surgical site. On our ward this is usually done on arrival from the operating theatre, then after one hour, after another hour, then after two hours, and then every four hours for the first day. Checks are done more often if needed. This is planned monitoring and does not necessarily mean that something is wrong. If enoxaparin (Clexane) is prescribed, the first postoperative injection on our ward is usually given about twelve hours after certain orthopaedic operations. The doctor decides the timing based on whether surgical bleeding is controlled, the type of operation, and any spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
- A clear path. Please leave a clear path to the bed on all sides. Do not put chairs or bags right next to the bed or in the way. The nurse and doctor must be able to reach the patient quickly, and the trolley with medicines and equipment must be able to get through.
- Fewer people is better. It is best if no more than one or two relatives are with the patient at the same time, if the ward rules allow it. When the staff come to the patient, please make room. If you are asked to leave the room, please do so. You can ask about the reason and ask questions later.
- Only with permission. After anaesthesia there can be nausea and vomiting, and a weak person may choke. After surgery, do not give the patient food or drink without the nurse’s permission. When the patient is awake enough, can swallow safely and there are no other restrictions, they may be allowed to drink and eat. Sometimes they start with a small amount of water and then move on to food. When and what is allowed is decided by the medical team.
- Rails and the button. Bed rails are raised when the staff decide, depending on the patient’s condition. They are not suitable for everyone and do not replace help with getting up. Do not raise or lower the rails yourself. The call button should be within reach. Get up only with the medical team’s permission, and the first time with the staff’s help.
- If something is wrong. If the patient feels sick, finds it hard to breathe, becomes unusually drowsy or hard to wake, suddenly becomes confused or very restless, the pain gets much worse, or the dressing quickly soaks with blood, call the nurse at once. Press the call button, and if the situation is serious, call the staff loudly. Do not give food, drink or medicines yourself, and do not try to lift the patient.
## Preventing infection in hospital: what patients and families can do
- Clean hands. Clean your hands with hand sanitiser or soap and water when entering and leaving the room, and before and after touching the patient. Wash your hands with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Hand sanitiser is usually by the room entrance or next to the bed. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.
- Avoid other patients' rooms. Do not walk around other rooms and wards of the hospital without need. Relatives with fever, cough, runny nose, vomiting or diarrhoea should not visit until they have recovered.
- Resistant bacteria. Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. If these bacteria are found in a patient, staff may introduce additional precautions, such as isolation, gowns and gloves, or special visiting rules. The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.
- Higher risk. Take extra care to prevent infection with open fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, external fixators (pins or wires pass through the skin into the bone), diabetes, and after complex or prolonged operations. The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.
- Tell the nurse straight away if: there is fever or chills; redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge from the wound; redness or discharge around the external fixator pin or wire sites, or around the IV catheter site.

## Video: Hip fracture — urgent surgery
## What kind of fracture this is
A hip fracture is a break in the upper part of the thigh bone (femur). A femoral neck fracture is a break in the narrow part just below the rounded head of the femur. A break immediately below the head is called a subcapital fracture.
- In many older people the bones become more fragile, including because of osteoporosis. That is why a fracture can happen even in an ordinary fall.
- A hip fracture usually causes pain in the groin or upper thigh. Most people cannot stand or put weight on the injured leg, and the leg may look shorter and turned outward. However, with some fractures, a person may still be able to stand or walk despite the injury.
- If a hip fracture is still suspected despite a normal X-ray, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is recommended. If MRI is not available within 24 hours or cannot be performed, computed tomography (CT) may be used.
- In most cases surgery is recommended. It helps reduce pain and start getting your movement back sooner. Sometimes, with a stable fracture or special medical circumstances, other treatment options are considered.

## From the emergency department to the ward
The diagnosis is made in the emergency department (ER) by an examination and an X-ray. Then you are moved to the orthopedic ward, where you wait for surgery.
- Until the medical team allows it, do not try to get up or walk on your own. Staff will help you change position in bed, take care of hygiene, and get ready for surgery safely.
- Pain is treated right away and checked regularly. If the pain gets worse, ask for pain relief. If ordinary medicines are not enough, a nerve block (a pain-relieving injection near a nerve) may be used.
- This time is used to check test results and, if needed, to get the body ready for surgery.
Source: NICE CG124, 1.3; AAOS 2021
## Skin preparation before surgery
Before surgery, staff will help clean your skin. Depending on hospital rules, special antiseptic wipes or other products may be used for this. This cleaning lowers the number of germs on the skin, but on its own it does not guarantee that infection will be prevented.
- Tell the staff if you are allergic to soap, antiseptics, or chlorhexidine.
- Do not shave the skin in the area of the surgery yourself.
- Tell the staff if you have a wound, redness, or skin irritation.
Source: WHO (SSI); CDC, 2017
## Why the Clexane injection
After a hip fracture, the risk of blood clots goes up. So the medical team assesses the risk of clots and of bleeding and prescribes prevention. One of the medicines is enoxaparin (Clexane). It may be given before surgery if the surgery is postponed; the timing of the dose depends on the surgery plan and the anesthesia.
- If spinal anesthesia is planned, there usually needs to be at least 12 hours between a preventive dose of enoxaparin and the injection in the back. After a treatment (higher) dose, the interval is at least 24 hours. If kidney function is reduced or there are other risk factors, the anesthesiologist may require extra safety measures. The surgery can also be done under general anesthesia.
- After surgery, clot prevention is restarted when it is safe. If spinal anesthesia was used, the first preventive dose of enoxaparin is given no earlier than 12 hours after the spinal injection; the length of the surgery, the risk of bleeding, and whether there is a catheter are also taken into account. The medical team decides the exact timing.
- If you take blood-thinning medicines at home, be sure to tell your doctor.
Source: AAOS 2021; NICE NG89; ASRA 2018 and 2025; Lovenox label (FDA)
## Food, drink, and medicines before surgery
- Because the surgery is urgent, its exact time may change. Do not eat or drink without permission from the medical team. Be sure to tell them when you last ate or drank. If the surgery is postponed, ask the staff when you are allowed to drink and how fluids will be given to you so that you do not get dehydrated.
- Tell the doctors about all your medicines, especially blood thinners: warfarin (Coumadin), apixaban (Eliquis), rivaroxaban (Xarelto), dabigatran (Pradaxa), clopidogrel (Plavix). They affect blood clotting and the choice of anesthesia; sometimes you need to wait before surgery or their effect needs to be adjusted.
- Be sure to tell the doctor if you take insulin or other diabetes medicines, especially empagliflozin or dapagliflozin. Say when you took the last dose. For urgent surgery, the doctor will decide which medicines to hold for now, how to monitor blood sugar, and whether ketones need to be checked. Some medicines raise the risk of a dangerous complication — ketoacidosis — even when blood sugar is not very high. Do not take or stop medicines on your own.
- Family members: bring a list of all the medicines the patient takes at home (or the packages themselves).
Source: ASA 2017 and 2023; NICE CG124, 1.2.2; AAOS 2021; ADA 2026, section 16
## Types of surgery
For a hip fracture without displacement, the surgeon may suggest fixing the bone with screws or a special metal device. Sometimes replacing part of the joint is considered. For a displaced femoral neck fracture in older patients, a partial or total joint replacement is usually recommended. A total replacement is considered based on how well you moved before, your general health, and how independent you are expected to be after recovery. A trochanteric fracture is a different type of fracture of the upper thigh bone, not a femoral neck fracture. It may be fixed with a plate with a sliding screw or an intramedullary nail (a rod inside the bone); for unstable types a nail is usually used. The final choice is made by the surgeon.

## When the surgery happens
- The aim is to do the surgery as early as possible — usually on the day of admission or the next day (NICE), within 24–48 hours (AAOS).
- In Israel, the Ministry of Health monitors the percentage of patients who undergo surgery for a femoral neck fracture within 48 hours of being admitted as inpatients. The nationwide figure was 89% in 2024.
- If dangerous health problems are found, the team gives the needed treatment and preparation at the same time, trying to avoid unjustified delay of the surgery.
Source: NICE CG124, 1.2; AAOS 2021; Israel Ministry of Health, report 2013–2024
## After surgery
- Getting up: usually the day after surgery, with a physical therapist, if your condition allows. Early movement helps prevent blood clots, pneumonia, and pressure sores.
- Putting weight on the leg: often you can put your full weight on it right away, as far as pain allows. But what is allowed for you specifically will be told by your doctor.
- After a joint replacement, special precautions when sitting, bending, and sleeping may be needed (usually for the first 6 weeks) so that the implant does not come out of the socket (dislocate). They depend on the surgical approach — the surgeon and physical therapist will explain which ones you need (AAOS OrthoInfo).
- Pain relief: regular, so that you can move and do your exercises.
- After surgery, clot prevention may continue after you leave the hospital. For hip fractures, injections of medicines that reduce blood clotting are often used. According to NICE, prevention is usually prescribed for a month if the risk of clots is greater than the risk of bleeding. The doctor decides the specific medicine and how long to take it. Do not stop the prescribed treatment on your own.
- A team works on your recovery: the surgeon, an internist or geriatrician, nurses, a physical therapist, and an occupational therapist.
- Before discharge, talk with your doctor about testing for osteoporosis, ways to prevent falls, and the plan for further rehabilitation.
Source: NICE CG124, 1.6–1.8; NICE NG89, 1.11.2; AAOS 2021; ACCP 2012
## Confusion (delirium) and how family members can help
In older people, sudden confusion can appear after a fracture and surgery: the person mixes up time and place and becomes restless or, on the contrary, sleepy. Confusion can begin even before the surgery. It is usually temporary, but sometimes it lasts longer and always needs to be assessed by the medical team. Tell the staff about it right away.
- Bring glasses, a hearing aid, and dentures.
- Visit more often, speak calmly, and remind the person where they are and what day it is.
- Tell the staff what the person was like before the injury — this helps them notice changes.
Source: NICE CG124, 1.8.3; NICE CG103

If you have sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, fainting, or severe difficulty breathing, call an ambulance right away at 101 ("Magen David Adom").

## Video: Knee Arthroscopy
## What this operation is
Arthroscopy is an operation on the knee joint, usually done through two or three small cuts a few millimetres long. Their number and size depend on the operation. An arthroscope — a thin tube with a camera — is passed through one cut, and thin instruments through the others. The surgeon sees the joint on a screen and can examine and treat damaged structures without opening the joint widely. This is why there is usually less pain and a faster recovery after arthroscopy than after open surgery (AAOS; NHS).
- It is often a day operation: you go home the same day or the next day.
## When it is done
- Some meniscus tears, especially after an injury, when the knee truly locks mechanically, or when symptoms continue despite treatment without surgery. Not every meniscus tear needs an operation: with age-related changes, exercises and other non-surgical treatment are often recommended first.
- Reconstruction of the anterior cruciate ligament.
- Loose pieces of cartilage or bone in the joint, some injuries of the cartilage and kneecap, inflammation of the joint lining (synovium) (AAOS).
- For knee osteoarthritis, arthroscopic washing out and “cleaning” of the joint is not recommended: it does not give meaningful long-term improvement (NICE NG226). If the knee is truly mechanically blocked, for example by a displaced piece of meniscus or a loose fragment inside the joint, the surgeon assesses separately whether this obstruction needs to be removed.
- Whether an operation is needed, and which one, is decided by the surgeon after examination and imaging (often an MRI).
## Before the operation
- General preparation rules — fasting, showering, medicines — are on the “Preparing for surgery” page.
- Tell your doctor in advance if you take blood-thinning medicines: they may need to be changed for a while. Do not stop them on your own.
- Do not shave your leg yourself before the operation: small razor cuts increase the risk of infection. If hair is in the way, the staff will remove it with clippers (NICE NG125).
- Your anaesthetic may be general (you are asleep), spinal (the lower half of your body is numb), or, in some cases, local (only the area being operated on is numbed). The choice depends on the type of operation and your health. The anaesthetist will explain the suitable option in advance.
## How the operation is done
- The joint is filled with sterile fluid so that its structures can be seen better.
- The surgeon examines the joint and, depending on the damage, may stitch a torn meniscus or remove the part that cannot be saved, take out loose fragments or repair or reconstruct a damaged ligament. Surgeons try to keep the meniscus whenever possible, because it helps spread the load across the joint and protects the cartilage.
- How long it takes depends on what needs to be done. Most knee arthroscopies take less than an hour. More complex procedures can take longer; overall, an arthroscopy takes about 30 minutes to 3 hours (AAOS; NHS).
- The cuts are closed with stitches or adhesive strips, and a dressing is applied, sometimes with an elastic bandage.
## After the operation
- In the first days keep your leg raised as much as possible — when sitting and when lying down. This reduces swelling (AAOS; NHS).
- Apply a cold pack wrapped in a cloth to your knee, usually for 20 minutes every 2–3 hours during the first few days, unless your care team advises otherwise. Cold helps reduce pain and swelling (NHS). Only use a cold pack once skin sensation has returned, and check your skin regularly. Never apply ice directly to the skin.
- Take your painkillers according to the prescribed schedule. Do not put up with severe pain — tell the nurse.
- Move your foot up and down and tighten your thigh muscles as the physiotherapist shows you: this keeps blood flowing in the legs (preventing blood clots) and maintains muscle strength.
- After a small arthroscopy, for example removal of a damaged part of the meniscus, you are often allowed to put weight on the leg quite early, sometimes with crutches. But after meniscus repair, ligament reconstruction or some cartilage operations the rules are different: weight-bearing or knee bending may be limited for a while, or a brace may be prescribed. Follow your own rehabilitation plan exactly — too much or the wrong kind of load can interfere with healing of the repaired tissues.
- After a short arthroscopy, not everyone needs medicines against blood clots: if the risk is low, early safe movement is usually enough. If the risk of clots is higher or the operation was longer, the doctor may prescribe preventive medicines, for example injections under the skin. They reduce the chance of blood clots but can increase the risk of bleeding. If they are prescribed, follow the schedule you were given (NICE NG89).
- If you go home on the day of the operation after a general anaesthetic, arrange in advance for someone to take you home and stay with you for the first 24 hours. For at least 24 hours do not drive or ride a bike, drink alcohol or make important decisions: the medicines can still have an effect even if you already feel well (NHS — general anaesthesia).
## At home and recovery
- Keep the dressing clean and dry. Ask the staff when you can shower. Do not soak your knee in a bath or pool until the wounds have healed.
- If non-absorbable stitches were used, your doctor will decide when to remove them — usually after 1–2 weeks. Dissolvable stitches usually do not need to be removed. If the wound is closed with special adhesive strips, do not pull them off before the set time — they may fall off by themselves or need to be removed carefully according to the instructions. Ask before you leave hospital when to remove or change the ordinary dressing: correct care helps prevent infection and damage to the healing wound.
- Do the exercises from the physiotherapist every day: they bring back movement in the knee and muscle strength.
- You can return to driving only after enough recovery: when you can use the pedals freely and confidently, can make an emergency stop quickly, and are not taking medicines that make you drowsy. After a minor knee arthroscopy, returning to driving usually takes about 2–3 weeks. It may take longer after a more complex procedure (AAOS; NHS). The exact time depends on which leg was operated on, the type of procedure and your recovery. Agree your return to driving with your doctor.
- Return to work and sport depends on what was done and on your job; the surgeon will tell you when.
## When to get help urgently
- If you suddenly become short of breath or have chest pain — call 101.
- A temperature of 38 °C or higher, chills, severe or increasing pain in the knee, marked or increasing swelling, redness around the knee, pus-like or bad-smelling discharge from the wound, new pain or swelling in the calf — get urgent medical help, the same day. These may be signs of infection or a blood clot.
- If you have severe, increasing pain that does not improve after painkillers, your foot becomes cold, pale or bluish, you have marked numbness, or it becomes hard to move your toes — get emergency medical help immediately. These may be signs of a problem with blood supply or another serious complication. Do not wait for a planned check-up.

## What this operation is
Kyphoplasty is a minimally invasive procedure for a vertebral compression fracture, most often caused by osteoporosis. Through a small puncture, guided by X-ray imaging, a balloon is placed inside the body of the broken vertebra and inflated. This creates a cavity and can sometimes partly restore the height of the vertebra. The balloon is then deflated and removed, and the cavity is filled with bone cement, which hardens.
- The goal is to reduce pain and strengthen the broken vertebra.
- Kyphoplasty may be considered if severe pain continues after a recent osteoporotic vertebral fracture that has not healed, despite appropriate pain treatment. Examination and imaging must confirm that the pain comes from the fractured vertebra (NICE TA279).
- The anesthesiologist chooses the type of anesthesia (general anesthesia, or local anesthesia with sedation).
- Kyphoplasty does not treat osteoporosis itself. After the procedure, talk with your doctor about testing and about starting or continuing osteoporosis treatment to lower the risk of new fractures.Vertebral compression fracture: the body of the vertebra is flattened. ## What this operation is
Laminectomy is a spine operation in which part of the bony arch of a vertebra (the lamina) is removed and, if needed, thickened ligaments and bony overgrowths are removed too. This creates more room for compressed nerve structures. In lumbar stenosis, the nerve roots are mainly freed; in other parts of the spine, the decompression may be aimed at the spinal cord.
- It is most often done for stenosis (narrowing) of the spinal canal in the lumbar spine, when pain, numbness, or weakness in the legs does not go away with treatment without surgery (Covaro et al., 2016).
- Sometimes, along with freeing the nerves, the vertebrae also need to be fixed together (spinal fusion), for example when the spine is unstable. The surgeon decides after your examination whether you need it.
- Getting up and walking usually start early, under the supervision of the ward team. The team decides exactly when.Spinal stenosis: the canal is narrowed and squeezes the nerves. ## Why
After spinal surgery you are usually taught to turn and get out of bed like a log: shoulders, hips and legs move together, without twisting your back. Your physiotherapist will show you the technique once the care team allows you to get up. When you first get up depends on the type of surgery and your condition. The first time, only with the staff.
## How to get up
- Lying on your back, bend your knees with your feet on the bed. Tighten your tummy muscles.
- Roll onto your side in one movement, shoulders and hips together. Do not twist or reach across your body for the bed rail.
- Lower your legs over the edge of the bed and, at the same time, push up with the elbow of your lower arm and the hand of your upper arm. Sit up with your back straight.
- Sit on the edge of the bed with your feet on the floor. If you feel dizzy, do not stand up and call the staff straight away; they will help you lie down safely. To stand, push up with your hands from the bed. Only once you are standing, hold the walking frame if you have been given one. Do not pull yourself up on the frame.
## How to lie down
- Sit on the edge of the bed, near the head end.
- Lower yourself onto your side using your arm and, at the same time, lift your legs onto the bed.
- Roll onto your back like a log.
## When to tell staff at once
New weakness or numbness in the legs, numbness around the genitals or anus (back passage), being unable to pass urine, or losing control of your bladder or bowels need urgent medical assessment. In hospital, tell the staff immediately. At home, go straight to the emergency department; for severe or worsening weakness, bladder or bowel problems, or numbness around the genitals or anus (back passage), call 101. If the pain suddenly gets much worse, also tell a doctor urgently.

## What this operation is
Fracture fixation (osteosynthesis) is an operation in which the surgeon puts the broken pieces of bone back in place and holds them together with metal implants: a plate with screws, separate screws, a rod inside the bone, or wires. Sometimes an external fixator is used — a metal frame outside the body, attached to the bone by pins through the skin. The fixation devices hold the bone pieces in the right position while the bone heals. This is why you can often move your joints and start recovery earlier (AAOS).
- A comminuted (multi-fragment) fracture means the bone is broken into three or more pieces. Such fractures can be unstable, but surgery is not always needed. The decision depends on where the fracture is, how far the pieces have moved, how stable the bone is and the condition of the surrounding tissues.
- In a complex comminuted fracture the surgeon does not always fix every small piece with screws. Sometimes a plate or rod holds the main parts of the bone in the right position, and the small fragments gradually heal between them. This helps preserve the blood supply to the bone fragments, which is needed for healing.
- If there is severe swelling or damage to the skin and soft tissues, the operation is sometimes done in two stages. First an external fixator is applied to hold the bone in the right position. Later, when the tissues allow, it may be replaced with an internal implant. This reduces the risk of wound problems. Sometimes the external fixator stays until the bone heals — it can also be the final treatment (AAOS; NICE NG37).
## When surgery is needed
- The surgeon decides after examination and imaging (X-ray, sometimes a CT scan).
- Surgery may be offered if the pieces have moved significantly, the fracture is unstable, or the correct position of the bone cannot be kept without an operation. For some fractures that go into a joint, surgery helps restore a smooth joint surface. An open fracture needs urgent surgical assessment and cleaning of the wound; the way the bone is fixed is chosen separately (NICE NG38; AAOS).
- Many fractures without displacement are treated without surgery — with a cast, a brace or a bandage.
- If there is a wound near the fracture, even a small one, the fracture may be open — the bone is not necessarily visible. This is dangerous because of the risk of infection. Do not try to wash out a deep wound yourself or push back a bone that is sticking out. Cover the wound with a clean dressing and call for medical help immediately on 101. In hospital an antibiotic is given into a vein as soon as possible, and the surgeon decides how urgently and how extensively the wound needs surgical cleaning (NICE NG37).
## Before the operation
- The injured limb is held in a splint or cast and kept raised: this reduces pain and swelling.
- The staff regularly check the colour and temperature of your fingers or toes, feeling and movement: this helps spot pressure on blood vessels and nerves in time.
- Tell the staff straight away if the pain suddenly gets worse or your fingers or toes become numb, cold, pale or bluish.
- If you have ever had an allergic reaction to metal, especially nickel, tell the surgeon before the operation. Some metal implants contain nickel, and this must be taken into account when choosing the implant (AAOS).
- General preparation rules — fasting, showering, medicines — are on the “Preparing for surgery” page.
- Smoking slows bone healing and increases the risk of wound infection (AAOS). If you smoke, talk to the team about stopping, at least during treatment.
## How the operation is done
- The operation is done under general or regional anaesthesia — the anaesthetist will explain the choice.
- X-ray images are taken during the operation to check the position of the bone pieces and the metal.
- The cut is closed with stitches or staples and a dressing is applied; sometimes also a splint or cast.
## After the operation on the ward
- Keep the operated arm or leg raised and move your fingers or toes: this reduces swelling and keeps the blood flowing.
- Put weight on the limb only as the surgeon has allowed. Too early or too much weight can move the bone pieces or break the implant. The physiotherapist will teach you to walk safely with a walker or crutches.
- Take your painkillers according to the prescribed schedule: when pain is under control, it is easier to rest and do your exercises. If the pain is severe, tell the nurse — the treatment may need to be changed. Do not add medicines yourself: some painkillers can cause drowsiness and dizziness, and in older patients this increases the risk of falling.
- After a broken leg, especially if you have to lie down for a long time or wear a cast, the risk of blood clots goes up. So the medical team assesses whether preventive medicines are needed. Not everyone gets them: the risk of bleeding is also taken into account. If a medicine is prescribed, take it on the schedule given, including after discharge if needed. Do not stop or add such medicines yourself (NICE NG89).
- Before you go home, make sure you have written instructions: how much weight you may put on the operated leg or arm, which exercises are allowed, which medicines to take, when to come for a check-up and whom to contact if there are problems. This matters because too early loading can disturb the fixation of the fracture, while too little movement can slow recovery (NICE NG38).
## At home and bone healing
- Bone heals over several weeks or months, sometimes longer. The time depends on where the fracture is and how complex it is, your age and general health. The pain may ease before the bone is strong enough. That is why you should come to your planned check-ups and follow-up X-rays: the doctor needs to see how healing is going and whether the bone pieces are still in the right position (AAOS).
- Keep the surgical wound clean and change the dressing as the staff instruct — this helps lower the risk of infection. Before you leave, ask when you may shower and whether the dressing needs to be protected from water. Ordinary stitches or staples are often removed after about ten to fourteen days, but the time can vary. Dissolvable stitches usually do not need removing. Do not take the dressing off or treat the wound yourself with products that were not prescribed.
- If you have an external fixator, before discharge the staff will show you how to care for the skin where the metal pins or wires go in. These sites need special attention, because infection can spread through them deeper, even to the bone. If there is increasing redness, pain, swelling or pus, see a doctor the same day. If a pin becomes loose, also tell the medical team without delay. Do not try to tighten the fixings yourself.
- Do the exercises from the physiotherapist: they bring back movement in the joints and muscle strength.
- Stopping smoking, eating well and taking your prescribed medicines help the bone heal.
- Plates, screws and rods are often left in the bone after the fracture has healed if they cause no problems. They do not need to be removed in everyone: that is another operation with its own risks. But some wires, screws and external fixators are removed after a certain stage of treatment. Your surgeon will explain whether and when your implant needs to be removed.
- Before an MRI scan, always tell the staff that you have metal implants. If possible, show your discharge letter or implant details. This is needed to check that the scan is safe: metal does not always rule out an MRI, but some implants need special conditions and may reduce image quality.
- If the fracture happened after a minor fall, especially in older age, talk to your family doctor about testing for osteoporosis. In this condition bones become weaker, so the risk of another fracture goes up. It is also important to prevent falls at home: clear obstacles from the floor, have good lighting and wear suitable, stable shoes.
## When to get help urgently
- If you suddenly become short of breath or have chest pain — call 101.
- Increasing severe pain that does not improve after painkillers, especially pain when moving the fingers or toes, numbness, cold, pale or bluish fingers or toes, or a cast that is too tight — go to the emergency department immediately. These may be signs of pressure building up in the tissues (compartment syndrome) or a problem with blood supply. Do not wait until the next day.
- A temperature of 38 °C or higher, increasing redness, swelling or discharge from the wound, new pain or swelling in the calf — get medical help the same day. These may be signs of infection or a blood clot.
## The first hours after surgery: how to help without getting in the way
- A special time. The first hours and the first day after surgery are a time when the patient is watched especially closely. Most of the time everything goes calmly, but sometimes help is needed very quickly. That is why the way to the patient and to the medical equipment must always stay clear.
- The nurse checks. After returning to the ward, the nurse regularly checks blood pressure, pulse, breathing rate, blood oxygen level, temperature and level of consciousness, and also examines the surgical site. On our ward this is usually done on arrival from the operating theatre, then after one hour, after another hour, then after two hours, and then every four hours for the first day. Checks are done more often if needed. This is planned monitoring and does not necessarily mean that something is wrong. If enoxaparin (Clexane) is prescribed, the first postoperative injection on our ward is usually given about twelve hours after certain orthopaedic operations. The doctor decides the timing based on whether surgical bleeding is controlled, the type of operation, and any spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
- A clear path. Please leave a clear path to the bed on all sides. Do not put chairs or bags right next to the bed or in the way. The nurse and doctor must be able to reach the patient quickly, and the trolley with medicines and equipment must be able to get through.
- Fewer people is better. It is best if no more than one or two relatives are with the patient at the same time, if the ward rules allow it. When the staff come to the patient, please make room. If you are asked to leave the room, please do so. You can ask about the reason and ask questions later.
- Only with permission. After anaesthesia there can be nausea and vomiting, and a weak person may choke. After surgery, do not give the patient food or drink without the nurse’s permission. When the patient is awake enough, can swallow safely and there are no other restrictions, they may be allowed to drink and eat. Sometimes they start with a small amount of water and then move on to food. When and what is allowed is decided by the medical team.
- Rails and the button. Bed rails are raised when the staff decide, depending on the patient’s condition. They are not suitable for everyone and do not replace help with getting up. Do not raise or lower the rails yourself. The call button should be within reach. Get up only with the medical team’s permission, and the first time with the staff’s help.
- If something is wrong. If the patient feels sick, finds it hard to breathe, becomes unusually drowsy or hard to wake, suddenly becomes confused or very restless, the pain gets much worse, or the dressing quickly soaks with blood, call the nurse at once. Press the call button, and if the situation is serious, call the staff loudly. Do not give food, drink or medicines yourself, and do not try to lift the patient.
## Preventing infection in hospital: what patients and families can do
- Clean hands. Clean your hands with hand sanitiser or soap and water when entering and leaving the room, and before and after touching the patient. Wash your hands with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Hand sanitiser is usually by the room entrance or next to the bed. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.
- Avoid other patients' rooms. Do not walk around other rooms and wards of the hospital without need. Relatives with fever, cough, runny nose, vomiting or diarrhoea should not visit until they have recovered.
- Resistant bacteria. Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. If these bacteria are found in a patient, staff may introduce additional precautions, such as isolation, gowns and gloves, or special visiting rules. The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.
- Higher risk. Take extra care to prevent infection with open fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, external fixators (pins or wires pass through the skin into the bone), diabetes, and after complex or prolonged operations. The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.
- Tell the nurse straight away if: there is fever or chills; redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge from the wound; redness or discharge around the external fixator pin or wire sites, or around the IV catheter site.

## Oxygen saturation during sleep after anaesthesia, and oxygen
## The number dropped in sleep
Your loved one has come back from surgery and fallen asleep. The number on the monitor dropped, the machine beeped, and the nurse gave oxygen through small tubes in the nose. Let's calmly look at what is happening. This number is called oxygen saturation: it shows how saturated the blood is with oxygen. It is measured by a sensor on the finger. In most adults it is usually ninety-five percent or higher. The care team sets the target level for each patient: if there is a risk of carbon dioxide building up, it may be lower.
## Why during sleep
Why does saturation drop during sleep? After anaesthesia and some painkillers, breathing can become slower and shallower. During sleep the airway can sometimes narrow or become blocked, especially in people who stop breathing in their sleep. Also, some areas of the lungs expand less well after anaesthesia. So when a person falls asleep, saturation may drop, even if it is higher while they are awake. After general anaesthesia this can happen even after minor operations. But a marked or repeated drop in saturation should not be considered normal. That is why staff check breathing, the patient's condition, and how easily the person wakes up.
## Nasal cannula
If saturation is low, the nurse checks the sensor and the patient, may wake them and ask them to take deep breaths, and if needed gives oxygen through a nasal cannula: two soft tubes in the nose. The flow is adjusted to keep saturation within the target range. Oxygen helps raise saturation, but it does not always fix the cause of the breathing problem. So the care team keeps checking breathing and the patient's condition.
## How you can help
How you can help. Stay close and stay calm. Do not remove the nasal cannula or touch the oxygen control. Do not give medicines, food or drink without the nurse's permission. When the person is fully awake, they can take a few deep breaths and cough, unless the nurse has said otherwise. Sometimes the monitor beeps for reasons that are not about breathing: for example cold fingers, hand movement, or a sensor that has slipped. Do not switch the monitor off yourself. Call the nurse to check.
## Call staff immediately
Call staff immediately, with the call button or by calling out loudly, if the person is hard to wake, is breathing very slowly, with pauses or shallowly, if the lips or face turn blue, or if there is shortness of breath, chest pain or sudden confusion. Do not wait for the lips to turn blue or for saturation to drop: on oxygen the number can sometimes stay normal even when breathing is dangerously weak.
## Oxygen safety
Oxygen safety. No smoking nearby, including e-cigarettes, and no open flames. Do not put petroleum jelly or oily creams on the face or lips. If the lips are dry, ask the nurse which water-based product can be used. Make sure the oxygen tubing does not kink or tangle around the feet. If the patient needs to get up, call staff first.
## When oxygen is stopped
As recovery from anaesthesia continues, the need for oxygen often decreases. But if saturation drops again, staff look for the cause. Oxygen is stopped by the care team's decision, when the patient keeps the target level without it.

## Why pain control matters
Some pain after surgery is expected, and it usually cannot be removed completely. But severe pain makes it hard to move, breathe deeply, cough and sleep, and early movement and exercises are important for recovery. The aim of pain relief is to keep the pain bearable so that you can do your exercises.
## In hospital
- The nurse will regularly ask about your pain on a scale from 0 to 10. Answer honestly.
- Tell the staff when the pain starts to increase; do not wait until it becomes very bad.
- If moving hurts, ask for pain relief in advance, before exercises or getting up.
- Several medicines are usually combined: paracetamol, anti-inflammatories (if they are suitable for you) and, for severe pain, opioids; sometimes a nerve block. The doctor decides the plan, taking your illnesses and medicines into account.
- Tell your doctor about all the medicines and supplements you take, and about any problems with painkillers in the past.
## Strong painkillers (opioids)
- They help with severe pain in the first days after surgery.
- Possible side effects: drowsiness, nausea, itching, constipation, dizziness. Tell the staff about them; they can usually be reduced.
- They are usually given for a short time and reduced as the pain eases, as your doctor advises.
- To prevent constipation: ask your doctor or nurse in advance whether you should take a laxative to prevent it. Drink enough if your fluids are not restricted, move as your doctor allows, and eat foods rich in fibre; if you have not opened your bowels, tell the staff.
## Without medicines
- Cold through a cloth, for as long as the staff allow.
- Raising the operated leg or arm.
- Rest between exercises and a comfortable position.
- Do this unless your doctor has said not to.
## At home
- Take painkillers exactly as prescribed; do not take more than the stated dose.
- Check the ingredients: do not take two medicines containing paracetamol at the same time.
- If you take blood thinners, do not start ibuprofen, naproxen or other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines yourself: the combination can increase the risk of bleeding.
- Do not combine opioids with sleeping tablets or sedatives without your doctor’s agreement: this can cause dangerous drowsiness and slowed breathing.
- Do not drink alcohol or drive while taking strong painkillers.
- Keep medicines out of the reach of children and other people; ask your pharmacist what to do with any left over.
- If you need painkillers for longer than expected, discuss it with your doctor.
## When to get urgent help
- The person is hard to wake, breathing becomes slow or shallow, or there is sudden breathlessness or chest pain — in hospital, call the staff immediately; at home, call 101.
- The pain suddenly gets much worse, is not relieved by the prescribed medicines, or other new symptoms appear — see a doctor urgently.

## Who this leaflet is for
For patients who have a planned (elective) operation in the orthopedics and spine department, and for their family members. This is general information: the individual instructions from your doctor, anesthesiologist, and ward are more important.
## Food and drink before surgery
Before anesthesia and sedation, it is important to follow the fasting instructions you are given: this lowers the risk of stomach contents getting into the airway. The time intervals are counted back from the start of anesthesia or the procedure. If you have diabetes, slow emptying of the stomach, or other risk factors, the anesthesiologist may give you a different plan. Follow the individual instructions from the ward.
General rules for adults without increased risk (ASA, 2017):
- a light meal (for example, toast and tea) — no later than 6 hours before;
- fatty or fried food, meat — no later than 8 hours before;
- clear liquids (water, tea or coffee without milk, clear juice without pulp) — no later than 2 hours before;
- milk (not breast milk) counts as food — no later than 6 hours before.
In some cases, clear carbohydrate drinks are allowed before surgery. You should not take special pre-surgery drinks on your own: follow the written instructions from the anesthesiologist and the ward (ASA, 2023).
## Medicines
- Do not stop or change your medicines on your own.
- Bring a full list of your medicines, including vitamins and herbal products (NICE NG5).
- Get separate instructions ahead of time for each medicine you take regularly: take it in the morning, skip it for now, or change the dose. It is especially important to agree on blood pressure medicines, diabetes medicines, and medicines that affect blood clotting. Morning pills that are allowed can be taken with a small sip of water (AHA/ACC, 2024).
- If you take medicines that reduce blood clotting (such as warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, clopidogrel, or aspirin), ask your doctor in advance whether any need to be stopped and when. Some, including aspirin in certain situations, may be continued. Do not stop them yourself. Your doctor will also decide whether any temporary replacement treatment is needed.
- If you take empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, or canagliflozin, tell your doctor ahead of time. According to FDA labels, these are usually stopped at least 3 days before planned surgery; ertugliflozin at least 4 days. Restarting the treatment is agreed with your doctor once your condition is stable and you are eating normally again.
- If you take semaglutide, tirzepatide, or other medicines for diabetes or weight loss, tell the anesthesiologist. Preparation and whether the treatment needs to be changed for a while are decided individually. Do not stop medicines on your own.

- On the evening before a planned operation, take a shower with soap and wash your hair. Put on clean clothes. If possible, shower again on the morning of the surgery (Israel Ministry of Health, circular 24/2001).
- What to wash with is in the section below.
- Do not shave the area of the surgery: shaving raises the risk of infection. If hair needs to be removed, the hospital will do it with an electric clipper on the day of surgery (NICE NG125).
- Take off jewelry, nail polish and artificial nails, and makeup.

## What a pressure ulcer is
A pressure ulcer is damage to the skin and the tissue beneath it caused by strong or prolonged pressure, especially combined with shearing of the tissues. It usually appears over bones: the base of the spine, the heels, hips, elbows, shoulder blades and the back of the head. Pressure ulcers usually develop gradually, but sometimes within just a few hours. Without treatment they can become open wounds.
## Who is at risk
People who move little or cannot turn by themselves; after surgery; older people; people with poor nutrition, reduced sensation, confusion or incontinence; people with thin, moist or damaged skin; and people who have had pressure ulcers before.
## First signs
- A lasting change in skin colour, for example a red patch that does not go pale when pressed.
- On darker skin the changes can be hard to see: the area may look darker, bluish or purple.
- The skin feels warmer or cooler, firmer or softer than usual.
- Pain or itching in the area.
- If you notice this, tell the nurse straight away.
## How to prevent them
- Change position regularly. The nurse will tell you how often, based on your risk. Usually lying on the back alternates with a slight tilt to the side supported by pillows; after orthopaedic surgery, positions are agreed with the staff. If the person cannot turn by themselves, the staff will help.
- Move yourself as much as you are allowed.
- Heels: a pillow under the lower leg so that the whole lower leg is supported and the heel does not touch the bed; sometimes special devices. The position is chosen with your post-operative restrictions in mind.
- Clean, dry skin. Do not rub or massage red areas.
- Sheets without creases or crumbs. When moving the person, do not drag them across the bed.
- Eat a varied diet and drink enough, unless you have a restriction. If appetite is poor, tell the staff.
- At high risk, the staff choose a mattress that spreads pressure and, if needed, a seat cushion. Position must still be changed regularly.
## At home
- Check the skin every day, especially over bones.
- Keep changing position and moving as advised.
- An area of skin that does not go pale with light pressure, unusual darkening of the skin, or a sore over a bone — contact a doctor or nurse the same day.
- Urgent assessment — if the skin around it is hot and swollen, there is pus, a fever, or the pain gets worse.

## What it is
- An abscess is a contained collection of pus in the tissues, like a “pocket” of pus.
- A phlegmon is a spreading inflammation of the soft tissues, usually caused by infection. Unlike an abscess, there may be no separate pocket of pus. Not every phlegmon needs surgery: treatment depends on how deep and severe the infection is and whether there is pus or dead tissue.
- A pus-forming infection can develop after a wound, a bite or a puncture, with a diabetic foot, or at a surgical site or around a metal implant.
- Signs: increasing pain, redness, swelling, hot skin, sometimes softening or pus coming out, fever and weakness.
## When and why surgery is needed
- With a formed abscess, the main treatment is opening it and draining the pus. If there is dead or infected tissue, the surgeon may remove it. This is called surgical wound cleaning, or debridement.
- Not every infection needs surgery. But with a deep abscess, dead tissue, or a suspected fast-spreading flesh-destroying (necrotising) infection, surgery may be needed urgently.
- Antibiotics are prescribed according to the type and severity of the infection. For a small surface abscess that has been fully drained, they are not always needed.
- With a spreading infection, signs of general deterioration, a markedly weakened immune system, a deep infection, or infection of the tissues around an implant, antibiotics are usually an important part of treatment.
- Having diabetes does not by itself mean antibiotics are always needed after opening any small abscess. However, a diabetic foot infection needs special attention and sometimes urgent surgery.
- When opening an abscess, the doctor may take a pus sample to identify the germ. If a deep infection after surgery or an infection around an implant is suspected, several samples are usually taken from deep tissues during the operation.
- Culture results help choose or change the antibiotics. With a severe infection, treatment is not delayed until the results arrive.
- Sometimes more than one operation is needed before the wound is clean. This does not necessarily mean treatment is going badly.
- With an infection around an implant (a plate, a screw or a joint replacement), the treatment plan is special: sometimes the implant is kept, and sometimes it has to be replaced or removed. The surgeon decides this together with you.
## The wound after surgery
- After the pus and dead tissue are removed, the surgeon decides how to close the wound. Sometimes it is left open or partly open to allow further treatment and drainage. In other cases it is closed straight away or at the next operation.
- This depends on how deep the infection is, the state of the tissues and whether more surgical cleaning is needed.
- There may be a drain (a small tube) to let fluid out.
- Sometimes a vacuum dressing is used (VAC, negative pressure wound therapy): a sealed dressing with a tube and a small machine that draws off fluid. Do not switch off the machine or touch the tube.
- The vacuum dressing helps remove fluid and keeps conditions right for healing, but on its own it does not replace surgical cleaning or antibiotics when they are needed.
- If the machine stops working or beeps, the dressing is no longer sealed, or fresh blood appears in the tube, tell staff straight away.
- Dressings are changed by the medical staff. The doctor decides which solution to use and how often to change the dressing, based on how the wound looks.
- You can ask for pain relief in advance, before the dressing change.
## What helps healing
- Well-controlled blood sugar, if you have diabetes.
- Good nutrition with enough protein; if needed, a dietitian will adjust your diet.
- Stopping smoking: smoking slows wound healing.
- Movement and limb position — as the doctor and physiotherapist instruct.
- If the infection is on the foot, especially with diabetes, the doctor may limit weight-bearing and prescribe a special offloading device. Do not put weight on the affected area against the team's instructions. If blood flow to the leg is poor, an assessment by a vascular specialist may be needed.
- Clean hands — yours, visitors' and staff's. Do not touch the wound or the dressing with your hands.
## Tell staff straight away if
- Fever, chills, severe weakness or confusion.
- Pain suddenly gets much worse, or redness or swelling spreads quickly.
- The dressing is soaked with blood or pus, or there is a bad smell.
- The skin around the wound darkens, or blisters or numbness appear.
- Especially dangerous: sudden confusion, fast breathing, sudden severe weakness, a marked worsening of your general condition, and pain much stronger than the wound's appearance would suggest. These may be signs of a severe infection, sepsis or flesh-destroying (necrotising) infection. In older patients and people with diabetes, a severe infection sometimes happens without a high fever.
- If a rash or itching appears after an antibiotic, tell staff straight away. If you have sudden swelling of the face, lips or tongue, difficulty breathing, severe dizziness or you pass out, call the medical staff immediately; at home, call an ambulance on 101.
## After you go home
- Dressing changes at home or at the clinic — exactly as set out in your discharge plan.
- If antibiotics were prescribed, take them for the whole prescribed time.
- Come to your follow-up with the surgeon on the appointed date.
- Seek help urgently (if severely unwell, call 101): high fever, quickly increasing pain, redness or swelling, pus, bleeding, or feeling unwell.
## Preventing infection in hospital: what patients and families can do
- Clean hands. Clean your hands with hand sanitiser or soap and water when entering and leaving the room, and before and after touching the patient. Wash your hands with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Hand sanitiser is usually by the room entrance or next to the bed. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.
- Avoid other patients' rooms. Do not walk around other rooms and wards of the hospital without need. Relatives with fever, cough, runny nose, vomiting or diarrhoea should not visit until they have recovered.
- Resistant bacteria. Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. If these bacteria are found in a patient, staff may introduce additional precautions, such as isolation, gowns and gloves, or special visiting rules. The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.
- Higher risk. Take extra care to prevent infection with open fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, external fixators (pins or wires pass through the skin into the bone), diabetes, and after complex or prolonged operations. The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.
- Tell the nurse straight away if: there is fever or chills; redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge from the wound; redness or discharge around the external fixator pin or wire sites, or around the IV catheter site.

## Rotator cuff repair
## The rotator cuff
- The rotator cuff is four muscles that run from the shoulder blade to the head of the upper arm bone. Their tendons wrap around it like a cuff. They hold it in the joint and help you lift and rotate your arm.
## A tear
- The tendon can tear after an injury or gradually, with age and strain. A tear can make lifting the arm painful and difficult, and pain at night is common. Sometimes a tear causes almost no symptoms.
## Surgery
- Most often the operation is done through small cuts, arthroscopically. The surgeon pulls the tendon back and attaches it to the bone with special anchors and stitches.
## After surgery
- After surgery, your arm is held in a sling, often with a pillow under the arm. Wear it as your surgeon told you, usually for several weeks, including at night. Do not take it off without permission.
- While the tendon heals, do not lift anything with this arm, do not lean on it, and do not raise it yourself until you are allowed. The repair could come apart.
- If allowed, move your fingers, open and close your hand, and bend and straighten your elbow and wrist. Shoulder exercises start according to your physiotherapist's plan.
- Many people find it easier to sleep half sitting up, with a pillow under the elbow. When dressing, start with the operated arm. When undressing, start with the other arm.
- Take pain relief as prescribed. Once feeling in the shoulder skin has returned, cold through a cloth can reduce pain and swelling. Use it only with the staff's permission, and keep the dressing dry.
- Keep the dressings on the wounds clean and dry. Wash the way the medical team explained.
## When to get help
- See a doctor the same day if the dressing becomes very wet, redness around the wounds increases, or there is discharge or a fever. After a nerve block, the arm may stay numb for a while. But if numbness is new or getting worse, the fingers are cold or pale, or severe pain does not ease after pain relief, get medical help immediately. If you suddenly feel short of breath or have chest pain, call the staff at once in hospital, and at home, call one zero one.
## Recovery
- The tendon heals slowly. Recovery usually takes several months. Do your physiotherapy exercises regularly. Do not smoke: smoking slows healing.

## What this fracture is
A fracture of the top part of the upper arm bone (next to the shoulder joint) is common, especially in people over 65 after a fall. There may be severe pain, swelling, bruising and limited shoulder movement. The diagnosis is confirmed with an X-ray, sometimes a CT scan.
## Treatment
- Many of these fractures heal without surgery if the pieces have not moved much: the arm is supported in a sling, followed by exercises guided by a physiotherapist.
- In the PROFHER study, in adults with a displaced fracture of the top of the upper arm bone involving the surgical neck, no statistically significant difference in shoulder function was found between surgical and non-surgical treatment over two years.
- For some severely displaced or complex fractures, the surgeon may recommend surgery: the fragments are fixed with a plate and screws, pins or a rod, and in some cases the joint is replaced. The surgeon chooses the treatment together with you.
## If you need surgery
Fasting, showering and medicines follow the general leaflet "Preparing for surgery". Do not stop blood thinners yourself — your doctor will tell you which ones to stop and when.
## At home
- Wear the sling for as long as your doctor says, usually several weeks; take it off for washing, dressing and exercises if this is allowed.
- Move your fingers, hand and elbow from the first days unless told not to. The shoulder is rehabilitated following your physiotherapist's programme.
- Bruising and swelling may move down the arm to the elbow and hand — this is normal and fades.
- In the first weeks, many people find it more comfortable to sleep half sitting up, with pillows.
- When dressing, put the injured arm in first; when undressing, take the uninjured arm out first.
- In the first weeks, do not lift or carry objects with the injured arm unless your doctor allows it; do not bear weight on it, push or pull with it. Drive only when it is safe and your doctor allows it.
- Movement returns over months, strength over up to a year.
- A fracture after a simple fall can be a sign of osteoporosis: ask your doctor about a bone check and preventing falls.
## When to get urgent help
- Your hand or fingers become numb, tingly, pale, bluish or cold, or the pain suddenly gets much worse — get medical help urgently.
- After surgery: fever, redness or discharge from the wound — see a doctor the same day.
- Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain — call 101 straight away.
## The first hours after surgery: how to help without getting in the way
- A special time. The first hours and the first day after surgery are a time when the patient is watched especially closely. Most of the time everything goes calmly, but sometimes help is needed very quickly. That is why the way to the patient and to the medical equipment must always stay clear.
- The nurse checks. After returning to the ward, the nurse regularly checks blood pressure, pulse, breathing rate, blood oxygen level, temperature and level of consciousness, and also examines the surgical site. On our ward this is usually done on arrival from the operating theatre, then after one hour, after another hour, then after two hours, and then every four hours for the first day. Checks are done more often if needed. This is planned monitoring and does not necessarily mean that something is wrong. If enoxaparin (Clexane) is prescribed, the first postoperative injection on our ward is usually given about twelve hours after certain orthopaedic operations. The doctor decides the timing based on whether surgical bleeding is controlled, the type of operation, and any spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
- A clear path. Please leave a clear path to the bed on all sides. Do not put chairs or bags right next to the bed or in the way. The nurse and doctor must be able to reach the patient quickly, and the trolley with medicines and equipment must be able to get through.
- Fewer people is better. It is best if no more than one or two relatives are with the patient at the same time, if the ward rules allow it. When the staff come to the patient, please make room. If you are asked to leave the room, please do so. You can ask about the reason and ask questions later.
- Only with permission. After anaesthesia there can be nausea and vomiting, and a weak person may choke. After surgery, do not give the patient food or drink without the nurse’s permission. When the patient is awake enough, can swallow safely and there are no other restrictions, they may be allowed to drink and eat. Sometimes they start with a small amount of water and then move on to food. When and what is allowed is decided by the medical team.
- Rails and the button. Bed rails are raised when the staff decide, depending on the patient’s condition. They are not suitable for everyone and do not replace help with getting up. Do not raise or lower the rails yourself. The call button should be within reach. Get up only with the medical team’s permission, and the first time with the staff’s help.
- If something is wrong. If the patient feels sick, finds it hard to breathe, becomes unusually drowsy or hard to wake, suddenly becomes confused or very restless, the pain gets much worse, or the dressing quickly soaks with blood, call the nurse at once. Press the call button, and if the situation is serious, call the staff loudly. Do not give food, drink or medicines yourself, and do not try to lift the patient.

Wash your hands with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Hand sanitiser is usually by the room entrance or next to the bed. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.
- Avoid other patients' rooms. Do not walk around other rooms and wards of the hospital without need. Relatives with fever, cough, runny nose, vomiting or diarrhoea should not visit until they have recovered.
- Resistant bacteria. Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. If these bacteria are found in a patient, staff may introduce additional precautions, such as isolation, gowns and gloves, or special visiting rules. The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.
- Higher risk. Take extra care to prevent infection with open fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, external fixators (pins or wires pass through the skin into the bone), diabetes, and after complex or prolonged operations. The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.

## Neck surgery from the front (ACDF)
Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is one of the most common operations for a compressed nerve root in the neck. Through a small cut at the front of the neck, the surgeon removes the damaged disc and, if needed, bone spurs, puts a bone graft or spacer in its place, and often holds the vertebrae with a plate and screws. The vertebrae gradually fuse.
Surgery is considered if pain, numbness or weakness in the arm continues or gets worse despite treatment without surgery. Increasing weakness in the arms, problems with walking or other signs of spinal cord involvement need urgent assessment, without waiting for that treatment to finish. For neck pain alone, surgery usually does not help.
There are other options too, such as an artificial disc or surgery from the back of the neck; your surgeon decides which is right for you.
## Preparation (including showering)
Fasting, showering and skin preparation follow the general leaflet "Preparing for surgery". If you smoke, talk to your doctor about stopping. Before surgery, let your family know that in the first days your voice may be hoarse and talking may be hard.
## Medicines
Do not stop blood thinners or other regular medicines yourself — your doctor will tell you which ones to stop and when. Details are in the leaflet "Preparing for surgery".
## Drain
Sometimes a thin tube (drain) is left in the wound to let extra fluid out. It is not always used — the surgeon decides. It is usually removed after a short time, once little fluid is coming out.
## After the operation
Usually within the first day you start getting up and walking with the team's permission; you can eat and drink when it is allowed and swallowing is safe. Many people go home after one or two days.
In the first weeks it is often hard to swallow solid food and your voice may be hoarse — this usually passes. Eat sitting upright, slowly, in small amounts.
Collar, driving, lifting and returning to work follow your surgeon's instructions. Fusion of the vertebrae takes several months.
## When to get urgent help
- Call 101 immediately (in hospital, tell the staff at once): difficulty breathing, neck swelling growing quickly, or you cannot swallow even your saliva.
- Go to the emergency department urgently: swallowing becomes clearly harder (especially after it had first improved), new weakness or numbness in the arms or legs, walking gets worse.
- Coughing or choking while eating or drinking — stop eating and drinking; in hospital tell the staff, at home get a medical assessment the same day.
- See a doctor the same day: fever, redness or discharge from the wound.

## Swelling after surgery
Swelling after leg surgery is common. It is usually worse in the evening and after activity, and eases gradually over weeks and months. It is important to reduce swelling and not miss warning signs.
## Raise your leg
How long and how often to raise your leg and use cold is decided individually. If your surgeon or physiotherapist has given you different instructions, follow them; the numbers below are only an example.
- Several times a day (for example 40–60 minutes, at least 3 times), lie down and rest your leg on pillows so that your foot and lower leg are above the level of your heart. After a knee replacement, keep the knee straight.
- Do not put a pillow under the knee only, unless your doctor says otherwise.
## Cold
- Wrap a cold pack in a towel; do not put it on bare skin.
- 15–20 minutes, then a break of at least an hour.
- Check your skin: if it is bright red, white or numb, remove the cold pack immediately.
- If feeling is reduced, circulation in the leg is poor, or you have diabetes with nerve damage, ask your doctor first.
- Do not place a cold pack on an open wound. Keep the dressing dry.
## Movement
- Bend and straighten your foot (“foot pumps”) several times an hour.
- Do your physiotherapist’s exercises.
- Do not stand or sit for long with your leg down; alternate walking, exercises and rest with your leg raised.
- Wear compression stockings as prescribed.
## When it is urgent
- Calf pain, swelling, heat or redness; swelling increasing quickly or not going down after resting with the leg raised; redness around the wound increasing or spreading, wound discharge, a high temperature or chills — see a doctor urgently.
- Sudden breathlessness or chest pain — call 101.

## Who this page is for
This page is for people who have already had a hip replacement through the anterior approach. It gathers general information about recovery and possible complications. The instructions from your surgeon, physical therapist, and medical team, based on your own operation, take priority.
Source: NICE, 2020 (NG157); AAOS OrthoInfo
## What the anterior approach is
The anterior approach means the surgeon reaches the hip joint from the front, usually using the gap between muscles. Even so, the soft tissues are still moved aside and shifted; it cannot be guaranteed that they will not be damaged. With other approaches, the joint is reached from behind or from the side. The type and parts of the implant are chosen separately, based on your anatomy and clinical situation, not only on the surgical approach.
According to the 2023 guidelines of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, there is no single approach that is best for all patients. Each has its own advantages and risks. The choice is discussed by the patient and the surgeon, taking into account medical factors and the surgeon's experience.
Source: AAOS, 2023; AAOS OrthoInfo
## The hip jointIllustration: Blausen.com staff (2014), WikiJournal of Medicine, CC BY 3.0
## Hip joint implantIllustration: BruceBlaus (Blausen Medical), CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia CommonsX-ray after hip replacement. Author: Photographer unknown (X-ray Image ID 3684), Public domain, source: Wikimedia Commons
## What to expect during recovery
In a 2023 meta-analysis of 24 randomized trials, patients after the anterior approach, on average, had better function scores at about 6 weeks compared with the posterior approach. At later time points the differences usually became smaller. The average length of hospital stay was about 0.33 days shorter than with the posterior approach, which is about 8 hours; this is an average difference across the studies, not a promise of earlier discharge.
These studies did not find a statistically significant difference in dislocations, fractures around the implant, or venous blood clots. This does not mean the risks are exactly the same: rare complications need larger studies.
Source: Meta-analysis of RCTs, 2023; AAOS, 2023
## How to recover
If your condition allows, rehabilitation and help with the first steps are usually offered on the day of surgery or within the next 24 hours. Your medical team decides how much weight you may put on the leg, whether you need a walker or crutches, and your exercise program. Increase your activity gradually, following their advice, and avoid falls.
Take the medicines prescribed after surgery, including medicines to prevent blood clots, strictly as written in your discharge papers. Do not stop them on your own. Wound and dressing care and the timing of your follow-up visit are also set by your individual instructions.
Source: NICE, 2020 (NG157); AAOS OrthoInfo
## Possible complications
After the anterior approach, numbness, tingling, or burning of the skin on the front and outer side of the thigh sometimes occurs. This may be due to irritation of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. It is responsible for skin sensation, so damage to it alone does not cause muscle weakness. In many people the sensations gradually lessen, but in some patients they remain.
Problems with wound healing, infection, nerve injury, fracture, dislocation, and blood clots are possible, as with other types of joint replacement. Some comparative reviews found more wound or nerve complications with the anterior approach, but the results depend on the studies and the experience of the surgical team. There is no basis to consider this approach completely safe or dangerous for all patients.
Source: Meta-analysis of RCTs, 2023; Meta-analysis, 2022; Systematic review, 2021; AAOS, 2023
## Movements and restrictions
After hip replacement, movement restrictions depend on the surgical approach, the stability of the implant, and the patient's characteristics. Studies after the posterior approach showed that standard restrictions did not convincingly reduce the risk of dislocation in the groups of patients studied; confidence in these data is limited. These results cannot automatically be applied to your operation.
After the anterior approach, some patients are allowed more movement, but this does not mean that everyone can right away cross their legs, sit on low chairs, or excessively rotate or move the hip out to the side. Before discharge, ask your surgeon or physical therapist about your specific restrictions and how long they last. Do not cancel them on your own.
Source: Korfitsen et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2025; RCT, 2020; AAOS OrthoInfo
More: At home after hip replacement: aids and safe movements
## When to get urgent help
Contact your surgical ward or surgeon the same day if you have a persistent or rising fever, chills, increasing redness, swelling, or pain around the wound, new drainage from the wound, or increasing pain in the area of the implant. If you cannot reach your surgical team, or your symptoms are severe or rapidly worsening, go to a hospital emergency department (ER). If you develop new pain, redness, warmth, or swelling in the calf or thigh, especially on one side, go to the emergency department (ER) immediately. These may be signs of a blood clot.
Call an ambulance immediately at 101 (Magen David Adom) for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or loss of consciousness. Sudden severe pain in the hip, a noticeable change in the position of the leg, or being unable to put weight on it may point to a dislocation or fracture: do not try to put the joint back in place or to walk through the pain; get emergency medical help right away.
Source: AAOS OrthoInfo
## Before discharge
Before you leave the hospital, check that you have written instructions: how much weight you may put on the leg, movement restrictions, exercises, medicines, wound care, the date of your follow-up, and a contact for questions. If any item is unclear, ask the medical team before you go home.
Source: NICE, 2020 (NG157)

## The first hours after surgery: how to help without getting in the way
- A special time. The first hours and the first day after surgery are a time when the patient is watched especially closely. Most of the time everything goes calmly, but sometimes help is needed very quickly. That is why the way to the patient and to the medical equipment must always stay clear.
- The nurse checks. After returning to the ward, the nurse regularly checks blood pressure, pulse, breathing rate, blood oxygen level, temperature and level of consciousness, and also examines the surgical site. If enoxaparin (Clexane) is prescribed, the first postoperative injection on our ward is usually given about twelve hours after certain orthopaedic operations. The doctor decides the timing based on whether surgical bleeding is controlled, the type of operation, and any spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
- A clear path. Please leave a clear path to the bed on all sides. Do not put chairs or bags right next to the bed or in the way. The nurse and doctor must be able to reach the patient quickly, and the trolley with medicines and equipment must be able to get through.
- Fewer people is better. It is best if no more than one or two relatives are with the patient at the same time, if the ward rules allow it. When the staff come to the patient, please make room. If you are asked to leave the room, please do so. You can ask about the reason and ask questions later.
- Only with permission. After anaesthesia there can be nausea and vomiting, and a weak person may choke. After surgery, do not give the patient food or drink without the nurse’s permission. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.

The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.

The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.
- Tell the nurse straight away if: there is fever or chills; redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge from the wound; redness or discharge around the external fixator pin or wire sites, or around the IV catheter site.

## Who this page is for
- For people after hip replacement and their families. Here are general tips on how to prepare your home and which aids make life easier in the first weeks. Your surgeon, physiotherapist or occupational therapist will tell you which restrictions you need and for how long.
## Prepare your home in advance
- Remove rugs, and carpets if needed, that you could slip or trip on.
- Clear walkways: move furniture and remove phone cords, extension leads and other obstacles.
- Put things you use every day at a comfortable height so you bend less.
- Place a small table at seat height next to your chair for the things you need.
## Chair and bed
- A stable chair with a firm seat, a backrest and armrests.
- As a guide, the seat and bed should be about 45–50 centimetres from the floor; the exact height is set individually according to your height.
- If you must not bend your hip more than ninety degrees, the seat should be high enough that your knees do not rise above the level of your hips.
- If furniture is too low, use a firm, stable cushion or special raisers that are securely fixed.
## Toilet
- If needed, a stable raised toilet seat (usually about 10 centimetres; the height is set individually).
- Securely fixed grab bars, usually angled, at about 60–90 centimetres, on one or both sides, so that sitting down and standing up is safe. It is best to agree the type and position with an occupational therapist.
## Bath and shower
- In the bath, sit on a special bath transfer bench, or shower instead of having a bath.
- In the shower, use a stable shower chair.
- A non-slip mat.
- Grab bars on the wall in the shower, by the bath and near where you wash. The sink and towel rail are not supports: do not hold on to them.
- A long-handled sponge, and soap and shampoo at a comfortable height.
## Dressing
- Get dressed sitting down.
- When putting on trousers, start with the operated leg, then the other leg. When taking them off, start with the other leg, then the operated leg.
- Helpful aids: a long-handled reacher, a sock aid, elastic laces and a long shoehorn.
## If your surgeon has given you movement restrictions
- Not everyone needs restrictions. They are given more often after the posterior approach; after the anterior approach, ask your doctor about your own restrictions. If you have been given them, the usual advice is (until your doctor says otherwise):
- Sitting: do not cross your legs; keep your knees apart; do not lean forward more than a right angle (90 degrees); do not pull the operated leg up to your chest; do not rest the foot of the operated leg on a high stool.
- Standing: do not bend more than a right angle, for example to pick something up from the floor; use a reacher. Avoid twisting the operated leg and turning sharply on it. Turn with small steps, moving your whole body. Ask your surgeon exactly which movements are restricted.
- Lying down: keep to the sleeping position you were advised. If recommended, use a pillow between your legs. Ask the medical team whether you may lie on your side, and on which side.
## Swelling
- Raising the operated leg can reduce swelling.
- To help prevent blood clots, the most important things are to walk as the team advises and to take the medicines you were prescribed.
- If the leg swelling suddenly gets worse, or you get new pain in your calf, redness or unusual warmth of the skin, get medical help urgently. If you suddenly become short of breath or have chest pain, call 101 immediately. A fever, discharge from the wound or increasing pain around the joint also need an urgent medical check.
## After discharge
- See your family doctor to continue treatment and follow-up.
- If needed, rehabilitation at home and/or adapting your home.

- Orthopaedic supply shops and websites.
- Databases of assistive devices: Azarim — azarim.org.il; Milbat — sheba.co.il.

## What this operation is
During the operation, worn or damaged parts of the joint are replaced with artificial ones, called a prosthesis (implant).
- The head of the thigh bone is replaced with a metal or ceramic ball on a stem, which is fixed inside the thigh bone.
- The socket of the pelvic bone is replaced with a cup with a liner.
The goal is to reduce pain and improve movement. ## How to prepare
- Shower. Take a shower the evening before surgery and, as the ward instructs, in the morning on the day of surgery. Use the soap recommended by the ward. If you were given an antiseptic product with chlorhexidine, use it strictly as directed, avoiding the eyes, ears, and mucous membranes. Wash your face with regular soap and your hair with shampoo. If you have ever had an allergic reaction to chlorhexidine, tell the staff before using it.
- Do not shave the surgical area. If needed, the staff will remove the hair with clippers.
- Remove jewelry, nail polish, and contact lenses.
- Smoking: it is best to stop as early as possible, preferably at least 4 weeks before surgery.
- If you develop a fever, signs of infection, or skin inflammation before surgery, tell the ward in advance. Do not wait until the day of surgery.
## Medicines before surgery
- Give your doctor and the anesthesiologist a complete list of all your medicines.
- If you take Eliquis, Xarelto, Coumadin, aspirin, or other medicines that affect blood clotting, ask your doctor in advance whether you need to stop the treatment temporarily and when. Some medicines, including aspirin in certain situations, are continued before surgery. Do not stop these medicines on your own.
- If you take medicines such as Jardiance (empagliflozin), Forxiga (dapagliflozin), or combination tablets such as Jardiance Duo or Xigduo, tell your doctor before surgery, even if you do not have diabetes. These medicines belong to a group called SGLT2 inhibitors. These medicines are also used for heart failure and kidney disease. Before planned surgery, empagliflozin and dapagliflozin are usually stopped at least 3 days beforehand, and ertugliflozin at least 4 days beforehand. This is to reduce the risk of ketoacidosis, which can occur even without a marked rise in blood sugar. Your doctor decides the exact plan for stopping and restarting treatment. Do not change your treatment on your own.
- Insulin and other diabetes medicines: get instructions from your doctor in advance about which medicines to take and in what doses. In type 1 diabetes, basal insulin is usually continued, with a dose adjustment as your doctor directs. You must not stop it on your own.
- If you take Ozempic or Mounjaro injections, be sure to tell your doctor and the anesthesiologist.
- On the morning of surgery, some medicines are taken with a small sip of water. Your doctor or anesthesiologist will tell you which ones.
- Fasting before anesthesia: after a light meal, at least 6 hours is usually required; after a fatty or heavy meal, at least 8 hours. Clear liquids (for example, water or tea without milk) are usually allowed until 2 hours before anesthesia starts. Some wards ask you not to eat after midnight. Follow the ward's individual instructions strictly, even if they differ from these general times.
## Drain after surgery
A drain is a thin tube that the surgeon sometimes leaves in the surgical area. Blood and fluid that collect in the wound flow through it into a closed bag or container.
- It is not always placed. The surgeon decides whether a drain is needed. After hip replacement, many people do without one.
- What it is for: to carry blood and wound fluid away from the surgical area. However, studies have not shown that routinely placing a drain after hip replacement lowers the risk of a hematoma (a collection of blood) or infection.
- If a drain is in place, bloody fluid may appear in the container. Over time the amount often decreases, and the color may become lighter. Sudden changes must be reported to the staff.
- When it is removed: often within the first 1–2 days after surgery, but the exact time is decided by the surgeon. A nurse or doctor removes the drain as instructed by the surgeon. You may feel brief discomfort during removal.
- Protect the tube: do not pull on it or allow it to kink. Keep the container below the wound without letting it pull on the tube, unless your care team instructs you otherwise. Do not try to reinsert or reconnect the tube yourself.
- Tell the nurse right away if: the tube has fallen out, moved, or come disconnected; the container is quickly filling with blood or the drainage suddenly became bright red; the flow of fluid suddenly stopped; fluid leaks around the tube; the drainage became cloudy or pus-like or has an unpleasant smell; pain, swelling, or redness got worse; you have a fever, severe weakness, or dizziness. Do not try to put the tube back or connect it yourself.

## Before you start
- Your doctor decides how much weight you may put on the operated leg. Your physiotherapist will show you how to walk in your particular case.
- This describes a standard four-legged frame without wheels; the technique is different for a wheeled walker.
- The first time you get up, only with the staff.
- Closed shoes that fit well, with non-slip soles.
## Frame height
Stand up straight with your arms relaxed at your sides. The handgrips of the frame should be at about the level of your wrist crease. When you hold the grips, your elbows are slightly bent and your back is straight. The staff or physiotherapist will set the height.
## How to walk
- Move the frame a short distance ahead. All four legs should stand on level floor.
- Holding the grips with both hands, step with the operated leg into the middle of the frame, not up to the front bar.
- Leaning on the grips, bring your good leg alongside.
- Take small steps. Turn slowly, in small steps, together with the frame.
## Sitting down and standing up
- Sitting down: walk up to a steady chair with armrests and turn your back to it until you feel the seat against the back of your legs. Move your hands from the frame to the armrests, one at a time, and lower yourself slowly. Do not hold on to the frame while sitting down.
- Standing up: push up with your hands from the armrests or the seat. Do not pull the frame towards you — it can tip over. Once standing straight, take hold of the grips.
## At home
- Clear rugs, cables and clutter; leave a wide path between rooms.
- At night: a light on the way to the toilet.
- In the bathroom: a non-slip mat and grab rails; if needed, a shower seat and a raised toilet seat.
- Check the rubber tips on the frame legs: replace them if they are worn or damaged.
- Do not use stairs or escalators with a walking frame. Your physiotherapist will show you how to manage stairs.
## When to get help
- You fall, the pain in your calf increases, swelling in the leg gets worse, or walking becomes harder — see a doctor urgently.
- Severe pain after a fall, you cannot get up, sudden breathlessness or chest pain — call 101.

## What happens in a worn joint
In osteoarthritis, several tissues in the joint change, including the cartilage and the bone. The cartilage that covers the joint can get thinner, and bony growths called osteophytes (bone spurs) can form along the edges of the bones. Pain, stiffness, and limited movement are different from person to person. When the cartilage is badly damaged, the bone surfaces can touch each other, and over time the shape of the joint can change. Having pain does not mean you definitely need surgery: there are treatments that do not involve surgery.
Source: NIAMS; AAOS OrthoInfo; NICE, 2022

## Taking pain medicine for years: what is the risk
Anti-inflammatory pain medicines — for example ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac — can reduce pain and improve how the joint works. But you should not take them for months without a doctor reviewing it. Possible complications include ulcers and bleeding in the stomach or bowel, kidney problems, higher blood pressure, and heart and blood vessel events. Some complications can happen even without earlier symptoms; the risk often goes up with high doses and long-term use.
Diclofenac rectal suppositories do not protect the stomach and bowel from these complications: the drug is absorbed into the blood. The official package insert for the suppositories also includes warnings about bleeding in the digestive tract.
It is especially important to talk with your doctor about the risk if you have had an ulcer or bleeding, if you have heart, kidney, or liver disease, if you are older, or if you take medicines that affect blood clotting, corticosteroids (such as prednisone), or certain antidepressants. Do not combine several anti-inflammatory pain medicines on your own.
If you need medicine for knee osteoarthritis, the guidelines first suggest an anti-inflammatory gel that you put on the skin. Tablets are used if the treatment applied to the skin did not help or is not suitable. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time. If you are prescribed anti-inflammatory tablets, your doctor should also consider stomach protection. NICE recommends offering a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that reduces stomach acid, while you take these tablets. This protection lowers some of the risks, but it does not make the treatment completely safe.
Source: AAOS, 2023; NICE, 2022; FDA, 2024; SmPC diclofenac supp., 2025
## Get help right away if you have these signs
If, while taking an anti-inflammatory pain medicine, you start vomiting blood, have black, tarry stools, see blood in your stool, faint, or suddenly feel very weak and bleeding is suspected — do not take the next dose and call an ambulance right away at 101 (Magen David Adom).
If you get severe or lasting stomach pain, stop taking this pain medicine and contact a doctor urgently. If you have chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, weakness on one side of the body, or trouble speaking, call emergency services right away.
Source: FDA, 2024; SmPC diclofenac supp., 2025
## When joint replacement is discussed
A referral to an orthopedic surgeon to discuss joint replacement is considered when pain, stiffness, loss of function, or a worsening deformity of the joint greatly reduce your quality of life, and treatment without surgery does not help or is not suitable. The decision to refer is based on a clinical assessment, not only on questionnaire scores or an image. Age, body weight, smoking, and other health conditions should not automatically rule out a referral on their own, but they can affect the risks and how you prepare for surgery. The final decision is made by the patient and the surgeon after discussing the expected benefit, the alternatives, and possible complications.
Source: NICE, 2022
## What waiting does
Waiting a long time with severe osteoarthritis can come with more limits on what you can do, less physical activity, and a lower quality of life. In a 2024 systematic review that included almost 90 thousand patients, joint function and quality of life got worse on average while people waited for surgery. However, the review could not reliably show whether the length of the wait itself affects the result after surgery. Some earlier observational studies linked worse function before surgery, or a longer wait, with less favorable results, but this does not prove that waiting is the cause.
You should not put up with pain for years without having your treatment reviewed again. This does not mean surgery is needed right away: the timing and whether surgery makes sense are discussed individually.
Source: Cooper et al., 2024; Fortin et al., 1999; Garbuz et al., 2006
## Modern surgery
In joint replacement, the damaged joint surfaces are replaced with artificial parts. Many patients have less pain and better movement after surgery. In a Canadian study published in 2010, 89% of patients after hip replacement and 81% after knee replacement reported being satisfied overall. These figures apply to the participants of that specific study and do not guarantee the same result for every person. Like any surgery, joint replacement has risks; the decision is made after talking with an orthopedic surgeon.
Source: Bourne et al., 2010

## What this fracture is
A fracture of the radius near the wrist is the most common arm fracture. The radius is one of the two forearm bones, on the thumb side. It often happens after a fall on an outstretched hand; in older people, especially with osteoporosis, it can happen even after a simple fall. There may be pain, swelling and bruising, and the wrist may look bent. The diagnosis is confirmed with an X-ray, sometimes a CT scan.
## Treatment
- If the pieces are in a good position — a splint, then a cast.
- If they have moved — they are often put back in place without a cut, with pain relief, then held still. Repeat X-rays show whether they have moved again.
- Surgery is considered if the pieces cannot be put back or held in a cast: the bone is fixed with a plate and screws, wires or an external frame. The surgeon chooses the method.
## If you need surgery
Fasting, showering and medicines follow the general leaflet "Preparing for surgery". Do not stop blood thinners yourself — your doctor will tell you which ones to stop and when.
## At home
- Keep your arm raised, with your hand above your elbow.
- Keep the cast and splint dry; cover them with a bag in the shower. Do not push anything under the cast.
- Move your fingers as early as possible, unless told not to.
- The cast is usually removed after 4–6 weeks; physiotherapy is often needed after that. Wrist stiffness eases over months; most people can use the hand for everything after about 3 months, and recovery may take about a year, sometimes longer.
- If the cast gets wet, cracks, becomes too tight or loose, rubs your skin, or there is discharge or an unpleasant smell from underneath it, contact your medical team without delay. Do not try to repair or remove the cast yourself.
- A fracture after a simple fall can be the first sign of fragile bones: ask your doctor about a bone density test and preventing falls.
## When to get urgent help
- Pain or swelling makes it difficult to move your fingers after a cast or splint is applied — do not wait a day: seek urgent medical help, the cast or splint may be too tight.
- Fingers numb or tingly, pale, bluish or cold; severe pain that does not ease or gets worse; severe pain when the fingers are gently straightened; weakness of the thumb or index finger — get medical help urgently.
- After surgery: fever, redness or discharge from the wound — see a doctor the same day.
## The first hours after surgery: how to help without getting in the way
- A special time. The first hours and the first day after surgery are a time when the patient is watched especially closely. Most of the time everything goes calmly, but sometimes help is needed very quickly. That is why the way to the patient and to the medical equipment must always stay clear.
- The nurse checks. After returning to the ward, the nurse regularly checks blood pressure, pulse, breathing rate, blood oxygen level, temperature and level of consciousness, and also examines the surgical site. On our ward this is usually done on arrival from the operating theatre, then after one hour, after another hour, then after two hours, and then every four hours for the first day. Checks are done more often if needed. This is planned monitoring and does not necessarily mean that something is wrong. If enoxaparin (Clexane) is prescribed, the first postoperative injection on our ward is usually given about twelve hours after certain orthopaedic operations. The doctor decides the timing based on whether surgical bleeding is controlled, the type of operation, and any spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
- A clear path. Please leave a clear path to the bed on all sides. Do not put chairs or bags right next to the bed or in the way. The nurse and doctor must be able to reach the patient quickly, and the trolley with medicines and equipment must be able to get through.
- Fewer people is better. It is best if no more than one or two relatives are with the patient at the same time, if the ward rules allow it. When the staff come to the patient, please make room. If you are asked to leave the room, please do so. You can ask about the reason and ask questions later.
- Only with permission. After anaesthesia there can be nausea and vomiting, and a weak person may choke. After surgery, do not give the patient food or drink without the nurse’s permission. When the patient is awake enough, can swallow safely and there are no other restrictions, they may be allowed to drink and eat. Sometimes they start with a small amount of water and then move on to food. When and what is allowed is decided by the medical team.
- Rails and the button. Bed rails are raised when the staff decide, depending on the patient’s condition. They are not suitable for everyone and do not replace help with getting up. Do not raise or lower the rails yourself. The call button should be within reach. Get up only with the medical team’s permission, and the first time with the staff’s help.
- If something is wrong. If the patient feels sick, finds it hard to breathe, becomes unusually drowsy or hard to wake, suddenly becomes confused or very restless, the pain gets much worse, or the dressing quickly soaks with blood, call the nurse at once. Press the call button, and if the situation is serious, call the staff loudly. Do not give food, drink or medicines yourself, and do not try to lift the patient.
## Preventing infection in hospital: what patients and families can do
- Clean hands. Clean your hands with hand sanitiser or soap and water when entering and leaving the room, and before and after touching the patient. Wash your hands with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating and whenever your hands are visibly dirty. Hand sanitiser is usually by the room entrance or next to the bed. You may politely ask staff to clean their hands before an examination.
- Wound and tubes. Do not touch the wound, dressing, drain, catheter or IV line, and do not remove the dressing yourself. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
- Leave others' things alone. Do not sit on other patients' beds, and do not touch their equipment, wounds or belongings.
- Avoid other patients' rooms. Do not walk around other rooms and wards of the hospital without need. Relatives with fever, cough, runny nose, vomiting or diarrhoea should not visit until they have recovered.
- Resistant bacteria. Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. If these bacteria are found in a patient, staff may introduce additional precautions, such as isolation, gowns and gloves, or special visiting rules. The measures depend on the type of bacteria and the situation. If staff give you special precautions, follow them carefully to help protect patients and families.
- Higher risk. Take extra care to prevent infection with open fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, external fixators (pins or wires pass through the skin into the bone), diabetes, and after complex or prolonged operations. The risk of infectious complications may be higher in these situations.
- Tell the nurse straight away if: there is fever or chills; redness, increasing pain, swelling or discharge from the wound; redness or discharge around the external fixator pin or wire sites, or around the IV catheter site.
