009	The first 48 hours
010	On our ward, the first planned dressing change is usually done about 48 hours after surgery. This is our ward's routine, not a single rule for all hospitals.
011	This is consistent with section 6.3.7 of the Israeli Ministry of Health inspection checklist: the first dressing change is carried out after 24–48 hours according to the local protocol. The CDC's 1999 guideline also advised protecting a closed surgical incision with a sterile dressing for the first 24–48 hours. That guideline has since been superseded.
012	The dressing is changed earlier if it is soaked with blood or fluid, has come loose or is dirty. A nurse does this.
013	Some special dressings are left on longer, as the surgeon decides.
014	How the dressing is changed
015	The nurse cleans their hands and changes the dressing using aseptic technique, with the right gloves and materials so the wound does not get contaminated. They check the wound for redness, swelling, discharge and edges coming apart.
016	In the first 48 hours the wound is cleaned with sterile saline if needed.
017	After 48 hours, a clean, dry, well-closed wound usually does not need any solution: the nurse checks it and puts on a clean dressing if needed. If there is discharge or crusting, the wound may be gently cleaned with saline.
018	If a dressing is needed after the examination, the nurse applies a new sterile dressing.
019	Do not touch the wound with your hands, and do not remove or adjust the dressing yourself.
020	Why not alcohol on the wound
021	Alcohol disinfects intact skin well (for example, before an injection or surgery), but it is not put on an open wound or a fresh incision: it stings badly, damages the living cells in the wound and can slow healing.
022	Iodine antiseptic (“Polydine”)
023	For an ordinary clean surgical wound that is healing with its edges together and without problems, international guidance advises against routinely applying antiseptics just to prevent infection.
024	On our ward, povidone-iodine (for example, Polydine 10% solution without alcohol) may be used when there is a specific medical reason, as the doctor or nurse decides, following the approved protocol. It is not a required step at every dressing change.
025	The solution can irritate the skin. If you notice redness, itching, a rash or burning under the dressing, tell staff.
026	Be sure to tell the doctor or nurse if you have had a reaction to an iodine product, have a thyroid or kidney condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or take lithium.
027	The different forms of the product (solution, alcohol-based tincture, soap) are not interchangeable. The alcohol-based tincture is not put on a wound.
028	Showering
029	You can often shower after 48 hours if the doctor or nurse allows it. Do not soak the wound: no bath, swimming pool or sea until it has healed.
030	When and how to wash depends on the operation and the type of dressing (some dressings are waterproof). Ask the staff.
031	What the research shows
032	A Cochrane review (2015) found no clear difference in wound infection rates whether the dressing was removed within the first 48 hours or later. The certainty of the evidence is very low, so the ward sets the exact timing for each operation.
033	A Cochrane review (2022) found insufficient reliable evidence to determine whether infection risk differs when wounds are cleaned with drinking water or saline. The review included different types of wounds. This does not change the recommendation to use sterile saline if a surgical wound needs cleaning during the first 48 hours.
034	NICE and CDC guidance advise against putting antiseptics or antibiotics on a clean stitched wound just to prevent infection.

046	Enoxaparin injections at home
047	Why you need the injections
048	After an operation on the leg, especially after a hip or knee replacement and after fractures, the risk of blood clots in the leg veins goes up. So the doctor may prescribe enoxaparin injections — for example Clexane or Crusia — after you go home too. Give them for as many days as your discharge letter says: do not miss doses and do not stop early, otherwise the protection against clots is reduced.
049	Inject yourself only if a nurse or doctor has shown you how to do it. If you cannot inject yourself, arrange in advance who will help: a relative or a community nurse, for example through your health fund (kupat holim).
050	Before your first injection at home, read the instructions for the exact syringe you were given. The technique below is for Crusia. Clexane and other prefilled syringes may have different safety mechanisms. If you were given a medicine or syringe that looks different, ask the nurse or pharmacist how to use it. Do not change the medicine or the dose yourself.
