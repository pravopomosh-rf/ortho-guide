004	When to get help urgently
005	In the hospital, tell the nurse right away. After discharge, contact the ward urgently or get medical care if you have a fever, increasing redness, swelling, or discharge from the wound, new severe pain in the hip, if the leg suddenly cannot hold your weight or changes position, or if you have swelling and pain in the calf. If you have sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, fainting, or severe difficulty breathing, call an ambulance right away at 101 ("Magen David Adom").

025	Who is this page for
026	For patients after a planned or urgent orthopedic operation (joint replacement, surgery to treat a fracture, or spine surgery) and their families. Individual instructions from your surgeon, anesthesiologist, physical therapist, and nurses are more important than these general recommendations.
027	Illustration: a ward bed, the call button, a walker, and the way home.
028	The first hours after surgery
029	Nurses regularly check your blood pressure, pulse, breathing, oxygen level (saturation), temperature, dressing, and feeling and movement in your leg or arm.
030	After major hip, knee or spine surgery, patients in our ward stay in bed on the day of surgery until the medical team says it is safe to get up. Staff decide whether to raise the bed side rails, based on your condition and your risk of falling. Rails reduce the risk of accidentally falling out of bed, but they are not suitable for everyone and cannot prevent every fall. Do not try to climb over or lower the rails yourself, as you could fall. If you need help or need to use the toilet, press the call button. Staff will help you or bring you a urinal or bedpan.
031	Tell the nurse if your pain suddenly gets much worse, if numbness or weakness in your arm or leg appears or gets worse, or if you have severe nausea, dizziness, difficulty breathing, or bleeding. Also tell the nurse if you cannot pass urine.
032	If your hand or foot suddenly becomes cold, pale or bluish — especially together with severe pain, numbness or weakness — call the staff immediately.
033	If the patient suddenly becomes confused, unusually sleepy, or stops recognizing family members, relatives must tell the staff right away.
034	You start drinking and eating when the medical team allows it.
035	Pain
036	To reduce pain, several pain medicines may be combined, for example paracetamol and, if needed, opioids. Anti-inflammatory medicines are not suitable for everyone: whether they are used depends on the type of surgery, the risk of bleeding, kidney function, and other medical conditions. The goal of pain relief is to reduce pain enough for you to move safely, rest, and do your prescribed exercises (NICE NG180; NICE CG124).
037	Do not put up with severe pain — tell the nurse. Do not take any of your own pain medicines without approval from your medical team.
038	Besides medicines, a comfortable position of the operated leg or arm and cold applied over a cloth may help — only as the staff allow; the dressing must stay dry.
039	Opioids can cause sleepiness, nausea, and constipation, and sometimes dangerously slowed breathing. They are taken exactly as prescribed and only for as long as they are needed. If the person is hard to wake up or their breathing has become slow and shallow, call the medical staff immediately (FDA).
040	Getting up for the first time and falls
041	It is important to start moving as early as possible after surgery, when the medical team allows it. After a planned joint replacement, rehabilitation usually starts on the day of surgery or within the first 24 hours (NICE NG157). In our ward, after major surgery you stay in bed on the day of the operation, so the first time you get up is usually the next day — with a physiotherapist or nurse. After surgery for a hip fracture, it usually starts the next day (NICE CG124). After other operations, the timing and how much weight you may put on the limb are decided individually. The first time you get up, staff will help you.
042	The first time, do not get up on your own: press the call button (call bell) and wait for staff. You may feel dizzy and weak.
043	To avoid falling: comfortable, closed, non-slip shoes, your glasses, the walker close by, a light at night; keep the call button and your phone within reach (NICE NG249).
044	Preventing blood clots
045	The team assesses your risk of blood clots; you may be given injections or tablets to prevent clots, compression stockings, or compression cuffs (inflatable sleeves that repeatedly squeeze and release your legs) (NICE NG89).
046	Compression stockings are only given after your legs have been checked: they may be unsuitable if you have disease of the leg arteries, reduced feeling (for example, from diabetes) or damaged skin. Do not wear stockings that were not fitted for you (NICE NG89).
047	If you are allowed to move your feet, regularly do the recommended ankle exercises (pull your feet up towards you, then point them away). They help keep your blood flowing, but they do not replace prescribed medicines or other measures to prevent blood clots.
048	If you get new pain or swelling in your calf, tell the staff right away. Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain may be a sign of a dangerous complication — call the staff immediately. If this happens at home, call an ambulance on 101 (Magen David Adom).
049	Breathing, drinking, eating, and bowels
050	Unless the medical team has told you otherwise, breathe deeply and cough several times an hour — this helps your lungs. A nurse or physiotherapist will show you how to do it correctly.
