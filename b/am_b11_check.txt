027	Contact the ward or see a doctor the same day
028	If your temperature reaches 38 °C or higher, stays high or keeps coming back, or you have chills, contact the ward where you had your operation or get medical care the same day. If you are feeling worse overall, get help no matter what the thermometer says (AAOS).
029	Redness, heat, swelling, or tenderness around the wound that is getting worse, pus or unusual discharge, a dressing that keeps soaking through, a bad smell, or the edges of the wound coming apart all need to be examined the same day (AAOS; NICE NG125).
030	Moderate pain, and slight swelling and bruising around the wound, are common after surgery and slowly get better. If you are not sure, call the ward.
031	The wound and preventing blood clots
032	Do not remove or change the dressing yourself unless the ward told you to. Whether you can shower depends on the type of dressing and the condition of the wound. Follow the instructions you were given at discharge (NICE NG125).
033	If you were prescribed medicines to prevent blood clots or compression stockings, follow the plan from your discharge papers. Do not stop the medicines on your own. If the stockings cause pain, strong squeezing, blisters, or a change in skin color, stop using them and contact medical staff right away. If you use stockings, check your skin every day (NICE NG89).

039	A tube by the wound
040	After surgery you may have a drain: a thin tube that comes out from the operated area and is connected to a container. Not everyone gets one. The surgeon decides whether a drain is needed.
041	Why it is used
042	Blood and fluid that collect in the wound after surgery drain out through the tube. If the wound becomes infected and pus forms, it may also drain through the tube.
043	Types
044	Most often it is a closed drain with a container: a concertina, a bulb or a bag. Some containers create gentle suction and draw the fluid out softly. A separate treatment is a vacuum dressing. It is sealed over the wound and connected to a device that creates suction. This is not the same as a drain tube.
045	What is in the container
046	The container usually holds blood-stained fluid. Over time there is less of it and it becomes lighter. The nurse measures the amount, records it, and empties or changes the container.
047	Moving with a drain
048	The drain is secured, and you can move with it. Get up only when staff say so. Keep the container below the wound, do not pull the tube, and make sure it is not kinked or trapped under your body. The container can be attached to your clothes, as the nurse will show you.
049	What not to do
050	In hospital, do not open or empty the container yourself. Do not disconnect the tube, squeeze the concertina or touch the dressing unless staff tell you to. If you go home with a drain, the nurse will explain how to look after it at home.
051	Tell staff straight away
052	Tell the nurse straight away if the tube has come out, moved or come apart; if the dressing around it is soaked; if a lot of bright red blood is quickly filling the container; if the concertina has expanded; if pain or swelling gets worse, you develop a fever, or the fluid becomes cloudy, pus-like or smells unpleasant.
053	When it comes out
054	How long it stays depends on the operation and the amount of fluid. Sometimes the drain is removed after one or two days, sometimes it stays longer. The surgeon decides when to remove it. A nurse or doctor takes it out: it is quick, it may be uncomfortable, and you will be given pain relief if needed. A dressing is put over the tube site.
055	After removal
056	In the first hours a little fluid may leak from the drain site; staff will change the dressing. At home, if redness is spreading, pus comes from the wound or the drain site, pain is increasing or you have a fever, get medical help as soon as possible.
