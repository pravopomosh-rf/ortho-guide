005	Drain after surgery
006	A drain is a thin tube that the surgeon sometimes leaves in the surgical area. Blood and fluid that collect in the wound flow through it into a closed bag or container.
007	It is not always placed.
008	The surgeon decides whether a drain is needed. After hip replacement, many people do without one.
009	What it is for:
010	to carry blood and wound fluid away from the surgical area. However, studies have not shown that routinely placing a drain after hip replacement lowers the risk of a hematoma (a collection of blood) or infection.
011	If a drain is in place, bloody fluid may appear in the container. Over time the amount often decreases, and the color may become lighter. Sudden changes must be reported to the staff.
012	When it is removed:
013	often within the first 1–2 days after surgery, but the exact time is decided by the surgeon. A nurse or doctor removes the drain as instructed by the surgeon. You may feel brief discomfort during removal.
014	Protect the tube:
015	do not pull on it or allow it to kink. Keep the container below the wound without letting it pull on the tube, unless your care team instructs you otherwise. Do not try to reinsert or reconnect the tube yourself.
016	Tell the nurse right away if:
017	the tube has fallen out, moved, or come disconnected; the container is quickly filling with blood or the drainage suddenly became bright red; the flow of fluid suddenly stopped; fluid leaks around the tube; the drainage became cloudy or pus-like or has an unpleasant smell; pain, swelling, or redness got worse; you have a fever, severe weakness, or dizziness. Do not try to put the tube back or connect it yourself.

019	The first hours after surgery: how to help without getting in the way
020	A special time.
021	The first hours and the first day after surgery are a time when the patient is watched especially closely. Most of the time everything goes calmly, but sometimes help is needed very quickly. That is why the way to the patient and to the medical equipment must always stay clear.
022	The nurse checks.
023	After returning to the ward, the nurse regularly checks blood pressure, pulse, breathing rate, blood oxygen level, temperature and level of consciousness, and also examines the surgical site. On our ward this is usually done on arrival from the operating theatre, then after one hour, after another hour, then after two hours, and then every four hours for the first day. Checks are done more often if needed. This is planned monitoring and does not necessarily mean that something is wrong. If enoxaparin (Clexane) is prescribed, the first postoperative injection on our ward is usually given about twelve hours after certain orthopaedic operations. The doctor decides the timing based on whether surgical bleeding is controlled, the type of operation, and any spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
024	A clear path.
025	Please leave a clear path to the bed on all sides. Do not put chairs or bags right next to the bed or in the way. The nurse and doctor must be able to reach the patient quickly, and the trolley with medicines and equipment must be able to get through.
026	Fewer people is better.
027	It is best if no more than one or two relatives are with the patient at the same time, if the ward rules allow it. When the staff come to the patient, please make room. If you are asked to leave the room, please do so. You can ask about the reason and ask questions later.
028	Only with permission.
029	After anaesthesia there can be nausea and vomiting, and a weak person may choke. After surgery, do not give the patient food or drink without the nurse’s permission. When the patient is awake enough, can swallow safely and there are no other restrictions, they may be allowed to drink and eat. Sometimes they start with a small amount of water and then move on to food. When and what is allowed is decided by the medical team.
030	Rails and the button.
031	Bed rails are raised when the staff decide, depending on the patient’s condition. They are not suitable for everyone and do not replace help with getting up. Do not raise or lower the rails yourself. The call button should be within reach. Get up only with the medical team’s permission, and the first time with the staff’s help.
032	If something is wrong.
033	If the patient feels sick, finds it hard to breathe, becomes unusually drowsy or hard to wake, suddenly becomes confused or very restless, the pain gets much worse, or the dressing quickly soaks with blood, call the nurse at once. Press the call button, and if the situation is serious, call the staff loudly. Do not give food, drink or medicines yourself, and do not try to lift the patient.
034	The first days after joint replacement: frequently asked questions
035	What is normal in the first few days
036	Pain, swelling, bruising and mild warmth around the operated joint are common after surgery. Swelling and bruising may extend down to the calf and foot and last for several weeks. The skin near the incision may feel numb. Weakness, poor sleep and constipation are also common. A slight rise in temperature can occur without infection, but tell your nurse about it. Report increasing pain, marked redness, unusual swelling or calf pain promptly. If you develop sudden shortness of breath or chest pain, call staff immediately.
037	Why pain relief matters
038	After surgery, pain medicines are prescribed according to an individual plan. Some are given regularly and others as needed. Good pain control helps you get up, walk and exercise safely. Tell your nurse about pain before it becomes severe, especially before physiotherapy. Rate your pain from 0 to 10. Take medicines as prescribed. If they do not help, cause side effects or you prefer not to take them, discuss this with staff. Do not change the treatment plan yourself.
039	What is a nerve block
040	To reduce pain, the anaesthetist may perform a nerve block. A local anaesthetic is injected near a nerve or group of nerves, often using ultrasound guidance. This temporarily reduces pain signals. Not every patient needs a nerve block. The anaesthetist decides whether it is suitable and which technique to use.
