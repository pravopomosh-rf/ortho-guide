001	If you have no fluid restrictions, drink enough; eat a balanced diet (AAOS).
002	After surgery, especially while taking opioid pain medicines, constipation is possible. Tell the nurse if you have trouble opening your bowels or have not had a bowel movement for several days. If you have severe abdominal pain, marked bloating, or vomiting, tell the nurse immediately.
003	The wound, drain, and catheter
004	Staff change the dressing. Do not touch the wound or remove the dressing yourself.
005	Keep the dressing dry. The medical team will tell you when you can wash and how to protect the dressing from water.
006	If you have a drain or a urinary catheter, do not pull on the tubes; tell the staff if a tube comes out, leaks, or causes pain.
007	Tell the staff right away if the dressing becomes heavily soaked with blood or other fluid, or if redness, swelling, or pain around the wound gets worse. After spine surgery, it is especially important to report clear, watery fluid from the wound or a new severe headache (CUH NHS; Eur Spine J 2015).
008	After spine surgery, new numbness in the area between your legs (perineum), increasing weakness in your legs, or problems controlling your bladder or bowels need immediate medical assessment (NICE NG127).
009	Where you go after discharge: rehabilitation
010	After a hip or knee replacement, a hip fracture or an amputation, patients from our ward usually go to a rehabilitation hospital. The medical team will discuss with you whether further rehabilitation is needed and the appropriate setting — in hospital, as an outpatient or at home — based on your condition. Arrangements will be coordinated with your health fund. There, the rehabilitation team helps you get up, move around safely — walking or using a wheelchair if needed — and manage everyday activities. This helps prepare you for a safe return home. If you would prefer rehabilitation at home, tell the ward team in advance. This depends on your condition, your home situation and approval from your health fund. If home rehabilitation is arranged, a physiotherapist will come to your home.
011	Before you go home (discharge), make sure you have in writing

022	“After surgery: warning signs”
023	If anything is unclear, ask for it to be explained again. A good way to check yourself is to tell the nurse in your own words how to take your medicines and what to do at home. Also ask who will help you book your follow-up visit and any tests arranged at discharge.
024	At home
025	Remove rugs and cords, and make sure there is light at night; grab bars in the shower and by the toilet, a raised toilet seat, and a stable chair with armrests can help (AAOS).
026	Increase your walking gradually and do the exercises your physical therapist gave you.
027	Only start driving again after checking with your doctor and when you can drive safely. Do not drive if you feel sleepy or while taking opioid pain medicines that affect your attention and reactions (AAOS).

040	Pain after surgery: how to keep it under control
041	Report pain honestly and early; do not wait until it is very bad.
042	Take painkillers exactly as prescribed; do not combine two products containing paracetamol.
043	Hard to wake, slow or shallow breathing: in hospital call staff at once, at home call an ambulance on 101.
044	Video: Pain after surgery: how to keep it under control
045	Why pain control matters
046	Some pain after surgery is expected, and it usually cannot be removed completely. But severe pain makes it hard to move, breathe deeply, cough and sleep, and early movement and exercises are important for recovery. The aim of pain relief is to keep the pain bearable so that you can do your exercises.
047	In hospital
048	The nurse will regularly ask about your pain on a scale from 0 to 10. Answer honestly.
049	Tell the staff when the pain starts to increase; do not wait until it becomes very bad.
050	If moving hurts, ask for pain relief in advance, before exercises or getting up.
051	Several medicines are usually combined: paracetamol, anti-inflammatories (if they are suitable for you) and, for severe pain, opioids; sometimes a nerve block. The doctor decides the plan, taking your illnesses and medicines into account.
052	Tell your doctor about all the medicines and supplements you take, and about any problems with painkillers in the past.
053	Strong painkillers (opioids)
054	They help with severe pain in the first days after surgery.
055	Possible side effects: drowsiness, nausea, itching, constipation, dizziness. Tell the staff about them; they can usually be reduced.
056	They are usually given for a short time and reduced as the pain eases, as your doctor advises.
057	To prevent constipation: ask your doctor or nurse in advance whether you should take a laxative to prevent it. Drink enough if your fluids are not restricted, move as your doctor allows, and eat foods rich in fibre; if you have not opened your bowels, tell the staff.
058	Without medicines
059	Cold through a cloth, for as long as the staff allow.
060	Raising the operated leg or arm.
061	Rest between exercises and a comfortable position.
062	Do this unless your doctor has said not to.
063	Take painkillers exactly as prescribed; do not take more than the stated dose.
064	Check the ingredients: do not take two medicines containing paracetamol at the same time.
