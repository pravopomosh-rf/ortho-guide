001	Your anaesthetic may be general (you are asleep), spinal (the lower half of your body is numb), or, in some cases, local (only the area being operated on is numbed). The choice depends on the type of operation and your health. The anaesthetist will explain the suitable option in advance.
002	How the operation is done
003	The joint is filled with sterile fluid so that its structures can be seen better.
004	The surgeon examines the joint and, depending on the damage, may stitch a torn meniscus or remove the part that cannot be saved, take out loose fragments or repair or reconstruct a damaged ligament. Surgeons try to keep the meniscus whenever possible, because it helps spread the load across the joint and protects the cartilage.
005	How long it takes depends on what needs to be done. Most knee arthroscopies take less than an hour. More complex procedures can take longer; overall, an arthroscopy takes about 30 minutes to 3 hours (AAOS; NHS).
006	The cuts are closed with stitches or adhesive strips, and a dressing is applied, sometimes with an elastic bandage.
007	After the operation
008	In the first days keep your leg raised as much as possible — when sitting and when lying down. This reduces swelling (AAOS; NHS).
009	Apply a cold pack wrapped in a cloth to your knee, usually for 20 minutes every 2–3 hours during the first few days, unless your care team advises otherwise. Cold helps reduce pain and swelling (NHS). Only use a cold pack once skin sensation has returned, and check your skin regularly. Never apply ice directly to the skin.
010	Take your painkillers according to the prescribed schedule. Do not put up with severe pain — tell the nurse.
011	Move your foot up and down and tighten your thigh muscles as the physiotherapist shows you: this keeps blood flowing in the legs (preventing blood clots) and maintains muscle strength.
012	After a small arthroscopy, for example removal of a damaged part of the meniscus, you are often allowed to put weight on the leg quite early, sometimes with crutches. But after meniscus repair, ligament reconstruction or some cartilage operations the rules are different: weight-bearing or knee bending may be limited for a while, or a brace may be prescribed. Follow your own rehabilitation plan exactly — too much or the wrong kind of load can interfere with healing of the repaired tissues.
013	After a short arthroscopy, not everyone needs medicines against blood clots: if the risk is low, early safe movement is usually enough. If the risk of clots is higher or the operation was longer, the doctor may prescribe preventive medicines, for example injections under the skin. They reduce the chance of blood clots but can increase the risk of bleeding. If they are prescribed, follow the schedule you were given (NICE NG89).
014	If you go home on the day of the operation after a general anaesthetic, arrange in advance for someone to take you home and stay with you for the first 24 hours. For at least 24 hours do not drive or ride a bike, drink alcohol or make important decisions: the medicines can still have an effect even if you already feel well (NHS — general anaesthesia).
015	At home and recovery
016	Keep the dressing clean and dry. Ask the staff when you can shower. Do not soak your knee in a bath or pool until the wounds have healed.
017	If non-absorbable stitches were used, your doctor will decide when to remove them — usually after 1–2 weeks. Dissolvable stitches usually do not need to be removed. If the wound is closed with special adhesive strips, do not pull them off before the set time — they may fall off by themselves or need to be removed carefully according to the instructions. Ask before you leave hospital when to remove or change the ordinary dressing: correct care helps prevent infection and damage to the healing wound.
018	Do the exercises from the physiotherapist every day: they bring back movement in the knee and muscle strength.
019	You can return to driving only after enough recovery: when you can use the pedals freely and confidently, can make an emergency stop quickly, and are not taking medicines that make you drowsy. After a minor knee arthroscopy, returning to driving usually takes about 2–3 weeks. It may take longer after a more complex procedure (AAOS; NHS). The exact time depends on which leg was operated on, the type of procedure and your recovery. Agree your return to driving with your doctor.
020	Return to work and sport depends on what was done and on your job; the surgeon will tell you when.
021	If you suddenly become short of breath or have chest pain — call an ambulance on 101.
022	A temperature of 38 °C or higher, chills, severe or increasing pain in the knee, marked or increasing swelling, redness around the knee, pus-like or bad-smelling discharge from the wound, new pain or swelling in the calf — get urgent medical help, the same day. These may be signs of infection or a blood clot.
023	If you have severe, increasing pain that does not improve after painkillers, your foot becomes cold, pale or bluish, you have marked numbness, or it becomes hard to move your toes — get emergency medical help immediately. These may be signs of a problem with blood supply or another serious complication. Do not wait for a planned check-up.

029	The bone fragments are aligned and fixed with a plate, screws, a rod or wires.
030	Keep the limb raised and move your fingers or toes; bear weight only as allowed.
031	Severe pain, numbness, cold or bluish fingers or toes: tell the nurse immediately or seek urgent medical help.
032	Fracture surgery: osteosynthesis
033	Fracture fixation (osteosynthesis) is an operation in which the surgeon puts the broken pieces of bone back in place and holds them together with metal implants: a plate with screws, separate screws, a rod inside the bone, or wires. Sometimes an external fixator is used — a metal frame outside the body, attached to the bone by pins through the skin. The fixation devices hold the bone pieces in the right position while the bone heals. This is why you can often move your joints and start recovery earlier (AAOS).
034	A comminuted (multi-fragment) fracture means the bone is broken into three or more pieces. Such fractures can be unstable, but surgery is not always needed. The decision depends on where the fracture is, how far the pieces have moved, how stable the bone is and the condition of the surrounding tissues.
035	In a complex comminuted fracture the surgeon does not always fix every small piece with screws. Sometimes a plate or rod holds the main parts of the bone in the right position, and the small fragments gradually heal between them. This helps preserve the blood supply to the bone fragments, which is needed for healing.
