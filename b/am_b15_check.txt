001	Raise your leg
002	How long and how often to raise your leg and use cold is decided individually. If your surgeon or physiotherapist has given you different instructions, follow them; the numbers below are only an example.
003	Several times a day (for example 40–60 minutes, at least 3 times), lie down and rest your leg on pillows so that your foot and lower leg are above the level of your heart. After a knee replacement, keep the knee straight.
004	Do not put a pillow under the knee only, unless your doctor says otherwise.
005	Cold
006	Wrap a cold pack in a towel; do not put it on bare skin.
007	15–20 minutes, then a break of at least an hour.
008	Check your skin: if it is bright red, white or numb, remove the cold pack immediately.
009	If feeling is reduced, circulation in the leg is poor, or you have diabetes with nerve damage, ask your doctor first.
010	Do not place a cold pack on an open wound. Keep the dressing dry.

012	If your doctor allows ankle movement, gently move your foot up and down several times every hour. Do not do this exercise if your foot is immobilised or movement is restricted.

015	Wear compression stockings as prescribed.

021	Sudden confusion after surgery (delirium): a guide for families
022	Delirium is sudden confusion: mixing up time and place, seeing things that are not there.
023	There is also "quiet" delirium: unusual sleepiness and sluggishness.
024	If the person suddenly behaves differently, tell the staff at once.
025	Video: Sudden confusion after surgery (delirium): a guide for families
026	What delirium is
027	Delirium is a sudden disturbance of awareness and attention ("acute confusion"). It develops over hours or days, it can change during the day and often gets worse in the evening and at night. Delirium is common in older people after surgery, especially after a broken hip, and it is usually temporary.
028	Signs
029	Mixes up time and place, has trouble understanding speech and concentrating.
030	Sees or hears things that are not there; restless or agitated.
031	"Quiet" delirium: unusual sleepiness and slowness, responds little — it is easy to miss.
032	Who is more at risk and why
033	The risk is higher in older people, in dementia or memory problems, in serious illness, and after a broken hip and other major operations. Causes and contributing factors include infection, pain, dehydration, some medicines, constipation or urine retention, poor sleep, unfamiliar surroundings, and poor eyesight or hearing. They often occur together.
034	What families can do
035	If the person suddenly behaves differently, tell the staff straight away. What you notice matters a lot.
036	Speak calmly, in short simple sentences; remind them where they are, what day it is and why they are in hospital; do not argue.
037	Bring glasses, hearing aids, dentures, familiar objects and photos.
038	Daytime: light, a clock and calendar in view; night: quiet and sleep. Help them drink and eat, if this is allowed.
039	What the team does
040	The team looks for the cause and treats it: infection, pain, dehydration, medicines, constipation or urine retention. If delirium causes severe distress or a risk of harm to the person or others, and calming without medication has not helped, the doctor may prescribe medicine at the lowest appropriate dose for a short time.
041	Afterwards
042	Improvement usually begins within a few days, but recovery can take weeks or months. The person may not remember this period well. If confusion persists after discharge, contact a doctor. If confusion suddenly appears or rapidly worsens at home, call an ambulance on 101 immediately.

047	Pressure ulcers: how to prevent them
048	A pressure sore is damage to skin and tissue from long pressure, usually over bones.
049	Change position regularly; the nurse will tell you how often.
050	A red patch that does not fade when pressed: tell the nurse at once.
051	Video: Pressure ulcers: how to prevent them
052	What a pressure ulcer is
053	A pressure ulcer is damage to the skin and the tissue beneath it caused by strong or prolonged pressure, especially combined with shearing of the tissues. It usually appears over bones: the base of the spine, the heels, hips, elbows, shoulder blades and the back of the head. Pressure ulcers usually develop gradually, but sometimes within just a few hours. Without treatment they can become open wounds.
054	Who is at risk
055	People who move little or cannot turn by themselves; after surgery; older people; people with poor nutrition, reduced sensation, confusion or incontinence; people with thin, moist or damaged skin; and people who have had pressure ulcers before.
056	First signs
057	A lasting change in skin colour, for example a red patch that does not go pale when pressed.
058	On darker skin the changes can be hard to see: the area may look darker, bluish or purple.
059	The skin feels warmer or cooler, firmer or softer than usual.
060	Pain or itching in the area.
061	If you notice this, tell the nurse straight away.
062	How to prevent them
063	Change position regularly. The nurse will tell you how often, based on your risk. Usually lying on the back alternates with a slight tilt to the side supported by pillows; after orthopaedic surgery, positions are agreed with the staff. If the person cannot turn by themselves, the staff will help.
064	Move yourself as much as you are allowed.
065	Heels: a pillow under the lower leg so that the whole lower leg is supported and the heel does not touch the bed; sometimes special devices. The position is chosen with your post-operative restrictions in mind.
066	Clean, dry skin. Do not rub or massage red areas.
067	Sheets without creases or crumbs. When moving the person, do not drag them across the bed.
068	Eat a varied diet and drink enough, unless you have a restriction. If appetite is poor, tell the staff.
069	At high risk, the staff choose a mattress that spreads pressure and, if needed, a seat cushion. Position must still be changed regularly.
070	Check the skin every day, especially over bones.
071	Keep changing position and moving as advised.
072	An area of skin that does not go pale with light pressure, unusual darkening of the skin, or a sore over a bone — contact a doctor or nurse the same day.
