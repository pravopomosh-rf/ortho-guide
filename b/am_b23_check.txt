011	What this fracture is
012	The ankle joint is formed by the lower ends of the shinbone (tibia) and the thinner leg bone (fibula), and by the talus bone of the foot. The bony parts on the inner and outer sides and at the back of the ankle are called malleoli: the lateral malleolus (outer side, end of the fibula), the medial malleolus (inner side, end of the tibia), and the posterior malleolus (back edge of the tibia) (AAOS).

014	A fracture of the lateral malleolus alone is often stable.
015	In a bimalleolar fracture, the lateral and medial malleoli are broken; in a trimalleolar fracture, all three are broken. These fractures are often unstable and often occur together with a dislocation (AAOS).
016	Sometimes the ligaments that hold the tibia and fibula together (the syndesmosis) are also injured.
017	Signs: severe pain, swelling, bruising, being unable to put weight on the injured foot, and sometimes a visible deformity.
018	The surgeon decides based on the examination and X-rays. Surgery is usually offered when the bone fragments are out of place or the joint is unstable: often in bimalleolar and trimalleolar fractures and in an unstable syndesmosis injury (AAOS; BOAST).
019	A stable fracture that is not out of place is often treated without surgery — with a cast or a special walking boot.
020	If there is a wound at the fracture site, even a small one, the fracture may be open. The bone does not have to be visible from the outside. This kind of fracture needs medical care right away (NICE NG37; AAOS).
021	Before surgery
022	The leg is in a splint or cast and is kept raised above the level of the heart: this reduces swelling (AAOS).
023	If there is a lot of swelling, the final operation is sometimes delayed to lower the risk of problems with the skin and the surgical wound. However, if the joint is dislocated or badly deformed, it may need to be put back in place urgently and held temporarily, without waiting for the swelling to go down. The surgeon decides when the final operation will be done (BOAST).
024	Tell the staff if the pain under the splint gets worse, or if you have numbness or tingling, or your foot becomes cold or pale.
025	Smoking, diabetes, and older age increase the risk of complications after surgery (AAOS). If you smoke, talk with the team about how to stop, at least during your treatment.
026	The surgeon puts the bone fragments back in place and holds them with a plate and screws or with screws alone; sometimes a rod is used. The syndesmosis is held with a screw or with a special cord with buttons (AAOS).
027	After surgery, the leg is usually held in a splint, cast, or boot.
028	After surgery on the ward
029	Keep your leg raised and move your toes.
030	How much weight you may put on your leg depends on the type of fracture, how firm the fixation is, and the condition of the soft tissues. Some patients are allowed to put weight on the leg early; others must avoid putting any weight on the injured leg for some time. Do not put more weight on your injured leg than your surgeon allows: follow the surgeon's written instructions. The physical therapist will teach you to walk safely with crutches or a walker (BOAST; WAX, The Lancet 2024).
031	When you have a fracture and cannot move as usual, the medical team assesses your risk of blood clots and of bleeding. If needed, the doctor prescribes a medicine to prevent blood clots. The choice of medicine and how long you take it are decided individually. If a medicine has been prescribed, do not stop taking it on your own (NICE NG89).
032	Tell the staff right away about: pain that does not get better with pain medicine; new numbness or tingling, or a pale or cold foot; a dressing that is soaked through; pain or swelling in the calf; sudden shortness of breath or chest pain.
033	Early healing of the bone usually takes at least 6 weeks, and complete healing 10–12 weeks; ligaments and tendons may need longer (AAOS).
034	Follow-up X-rays are done at the times your doctor sets.
035	Keep the cast and dressing dry unless the medical team has told you otherwise. Do not put anything inside the cast, and do not try to cut or remove it yourself. If the cast gets wet, breaks, rubs badly, or there is a bad smell or discharge from under it, contact your treating team or go to the emergency department without delay (NHS).
036	Swelling and stiffness in the ankle may last for several months (AAOS).
037	Most people return to their usual activities 3–4 months after the injury; full recovery after some fractures can take up to 2 years (AAOS).
038	You may return to driving once your doctor agrees, when you can control the car safely and make an emergency stop without pain or limitation. Do not drive if a splint, cast, or boot on your right leg, or medicines that make you sleepy, stop you from driving safely. For a fracture of the right ankle, AAOS gives an approximate time of 9–12 weeks, but this is not a required minimum for everyone.
039	Screws and plates often stay in the bone. Whether they need to be removed is decided by the surgeon.
040	If at home you suddenly become short of breath or have chest pain — call an ambulance on 101.
041	If your foot becomes cold, pale, or blue, you have marked numbness, the pain suddenly gets much worse and does not ease with your prescribed pain medicine, or the cast suddenly feels very tight — go to the emergency department (ER) right away. Do not wait until the next day.
042	If you have a temperature of 38 °C or higher, redness or discharge from the wound, or new pain or swelling in the calf, you need a medical check on the same day (see the page "After surgery: warning signs").
