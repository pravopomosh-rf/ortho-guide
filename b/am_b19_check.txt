009	When joint replacement is discussed
010	A referral to an orthopedic surgeon to discuss joint replacement is considered when pain, stiffness, loss of function, or a worsening deformity of the joint greatly reduce your quality of life, and treatment without surgery does not help or is not suitable. The decision to refer is based on a clinical assessment, not only on questionnaire scores or an image. Age, body weight, smoking, and other health conditions should not automatically rule out a referral on their own, but they can affect the risks and how you prepare for surgery. The final decision is made by the patient and the surgeon after discussing the expected benefit, the alternatives, and possible complications.
011	Source: NICE, 2022
012	What waiting does
013	Waiting a long time with severe osteoarthritis can come with more limits on what you can do, less physical activity, and a lower quality of life. In a 2024 systematic review that included almost 90,000 patients, joint function and quality of life got worse on average while people waited for surgery. However, the review could not reliably show whether the length of the wait itself affects the result after surgery. Some earlier observational studies linked worse function before surgery, or a longer wait, with less favorable results, but this does not prove that waiting is the cause.
014	You should not put up with pain for years without having your treatment reviewed again. This does not mean surgery is needed right away: the timing and whether surgery makes sense are discussed individually.
015	Source: Cooper et al., 2024; Fortin et al., 1999; Garbuz et al., 2006
016	Modern surgery
017	In joint replacement, the damaged joint surfaces are replaced with artificial parts. Many patients have less pain and better movement after surgery. In a Canadian study published in 2010, 89% of patients after hip replacement and 81% after knee replacement reported being satisfied overall. These figures apply to the participants of that specific study and do not guarantee the same result for every person. Like any surgery, joint replacement has risks; the decision is made after talking with an orthopedic surgeon.
018	Source: Bourne et al., 2010

029	CPE and VRE are bacteria resistant to some antibiotics.
030	A positive swab often means carriage, not illness.
031	Wash your hands with soap and water after the toilet; follow the isolation rules.
032	What CPE and VRE are
033	CPE and VRE are groups of bacteria that are resistant to certain antibiotics.
034	CPE are bacteria, such as E. coli or Klebsiella, that produce enzymes able to break down antibiotics of the carbapenem group.
035	VRE are enterococci that are resistant to the antibiotic vancomycin.
036	They can live in the gut without causing illness. This is called carriage. A person can feel completely well, but the bacteria can be passed on to other patients.
037	In hospital they spread mainly by contact: through contaminated hands, medical equipment and surrounding objects. If these bacteria cause an infection, finding an effective antibiotic can be harder.
038	How the swab is taken
039	A nurse or another trained staff member gently inserts a sterile swab a short way into the anus to take a sample from the rectum.
040	It takes only a short time. It usually does not hurt, but you may feel brief discomfort.
041	For a reliable test, a small amount of stool should be on the swab. In some cases a stool sample is used instead.
042	Why on admission and during the hospital stay
043	In Israel there are rules for finding carriers of resistant bacteria in hospitals.
044	For CPE, screening is especially important after a transfer from another healthcare facility, after a recent hospital stay, after contact with a known carrier, and in high-risk wards.
045	For VRE, the hospital infection control team decides which groups of patients are tested on admission and when repeat tests are needed.
046	On our ward the swab is usually taken on admission and repeated after about fourteen days if the patient is still in hospital. This is a local routine, not a uniform requirement for all hospitals in Israel.
