001	Before you leave the hospital, check that you have written instructions: how much weight you may put on the leg, movement restrictions, exercises, medicines, wound care, the date of your follow-up, and a contact for questions. If any item is unclear, ask the medical team before you go home.
002	Source: NICE, 2020 (NG157)
003	Contact your surgical ward or surgeon the same day if you have a persistent or rising fever, chills, increasing redness, swelling, or pain around the wound, new drainage from the wound, or increasing pain in the area of the implant. If you cannot reach your surgical team, or your symptoms are severe or rapidly worsening, go to a hospital emergency department (ER). If you develop new pain, swelling, redness or warmth in your calf or thigh, especially on one side, seek urgent medical assessment the same day. These may be signs of a blood clot. If immediate medical assessment is unavailable, go to the emergency department. Call an ambulance on 101 for sudden shortness of breath or chest pain.
004	Call an ambulance immediately at 101 (Magen David Adom) for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or loss of consciousness. Sudden severe pain in the hip, a noticeable change in the position of the leg, or being unable to put weight on it may point to a dislocation or fracture: do not try to put the joint back in place or to walk through the pain; get emergency medical help right away.

015	Feeling that the leg is still there is common: remember this before you get up.
016	Do not remove the dressing yourself; before discharge, ask who will change it.
017	Redness, increasing swelling, discharge or pus from the stump: see a doctor the same day.
018	An amputation is the removal of part of a limb when it cannot be saved: for example, because of a severe infection or gangrene, often linked to diabetes or poor blood supply, or after a serious injury. The surgeon removes the damaged part, shapes the bones and covers them with muscle and skin to form a stump. The surgeon chooses the level of amputation after an assessment. The operation is done under general anaesthetic or under a spinal or epidural anaesthetic.
019	If the operation is planned, you will be assessed: your heart, blood vessels, lungs and your other leg are checked. The team may discuss rehabilitation, the possibility of a prosthesis and your home situation with you. Fasting, showering and medicines follow the general leaflet "Preparing for surgery". You can ask to meet someone who has had a similar operation.
020	Do not stop blood thinners or other regular medicines yourself — your doctor will tell you which ones to stop and when. Take painkillers as prescribed: some regularly, others only when needed. Before a physiotherapy session, check with your nurse in advance whether you need extra pain relief. Tell your nurse about any pain, including phantom pain.

022	Drain and wound
023	Sometimes a drain is left in — the surgeon decides. In hospital, the medical staff change the dressing. Before discharge, check who will do your dressings afterwards and when. Do not remove the dressing yourself against the instructions you were given. Non-dissolvable stitches are removed by staff, usually after two to three weeks.
024	Many people feel as if the amputated leg is still there (phantom sensations). In the first months it is easy to forget that the leg is gone, especially at night. Before getting up, stop and remember this; in hospital, press the call button.
025	After a below-knee amputation, do not put a pillow under the stump and do not bend the bed under the knee — this helps keep the knee straight. In a chair, the stump rests on a stump board.
026	After an above-knee amputation, do not keep the hip bent for long periods, and do not keep a pillow under the stump or between the thighs all the time. Lie on your stomach or do hip-straightening exercises only if the rehabilitation team allows it.
027	After removal of a toe or part of the foot, do not decide by yourself how much weight to put on the leg. The doctor may forbid weight-bearing for a while, allow partial weight-bearing, or allow walking with weight on the heel. Sometimes a special post-operative shoe is needed to take pressure off the wound: use it and your walking aids exactly as the physiotherapist showed you. Do not walk in an ordinary shoe without permission.
028	Rehabilitation usually starts the next day: exercises in bed, then moving safely into a wheelchair. Hopping on one leg with crutches is not advised.
029	Once the wound has healed, wash the stump daily with mild unscented soap and dry it carefully; check it every day.
030	Protect your other leg, especially if you have diabetes: check your foot daily, wear comfortable shoes and see a foot specialist regularly.
031	If the person spends a lot of time in bed, staff should use an appropriate device to take pressure off the heel of the remaining leg and help prevent a pressure ulcer.
032	With diabetes, blood sugar after surgery can go up but can also drop, especially if the person is eating little and takes insulin or some diabetes tablets. If the patient skips a meal, tell the staff straight away. Also tell them immediately if the patient develops unusual weakness, sweating, shaking, dizziness, confusion or drowsiness. Do not change medicine doses yourself.
033	In older people, sudden confusion (delirium) sometimes appears after a major operation: restlessness, disorientation — or the opposite, the person becomes quiet and drowsy. If you notice a sudden change, tell the nurse at once. More on the page

035	A prosthesis is not right for everyone: it depends on healing, muscle strength and general health. Without a prosthesis, you can still become independent with a wheelchair.
