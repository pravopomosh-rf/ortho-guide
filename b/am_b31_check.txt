015	With diabetes the foot feels pain less: a wound is easy to miss.
016	Do not stand on the affected foot if told not to; do not change the dressing yourself.
017	Fever, chills, fast-spreading redness, pus or darkening skin: tell the staff at once.
018	Why it is dangerous
019	Over time, high blood sugar damages the nerves: the foot feels pain, heat and tight shoes less well. A callus, a burn or a small stone in the shoe can go unnoticed.
020	Blood flow to the legs is often reduced, so wounds heal slowly.
021	Infection can spread quickly deep into the foot, down to the bone. Without treatment this can end in amputation.
022	How we treat it on the ward
023	The doctor examines the wound and assesses its depth, signs of infection, feeling in the foot and blood flow in both legs. Blood tests are done when needed.
024	If a bone infection is suspected, an X-ray is usually done first; if the diagnosis is still unclear, an MRI may be needed. Blood flow is checked with a vascular ultrasound and by measuring the pressure in the arteries of the leg or toes.
025	Not every wound needs a culture. If there are signs of infection, the doctor may take a tissue sample from deep in the wound after cleaning it, to identify the bacteria and choose an antibiotic.
026	Antibiotics are given when there is an infection. If the wound is not infected, they do not speed up healing. In a severe infection, treatment starts straight away without waiting for the culture result.
027	Wound cleaning: when needed, a specialist removes dead tissue. If blood flow is very poor, the approach is chosen according to the state of the blood vessels.
028	Taking pressure off the foot: for the wound to heal, pressure on it must be reduced — with a special brace, a cast, a shoe or mobility aids. Sometimes you must not stand on the foot at all; sometimes it is allowed only in a special device. The medical team will explain the rules.
029	If blood flow is reduced, a vascular specialist must assess it; sometimes a procedure to restore blood flow is needed. Blood sugar is also controlled, and infection and wound-care specialists are involved when needed.
030	What the patient and family can do
031	Do not stand on the affected foot if you have been told not to. Even a few barefoot steps can damage the wound.
032	Do not remove, change or wet the dressing unless the medical staff tell you to. If the dressing gets wet, dirty or slips, tell the nurse.
033	Good nutrition with enough calories and protein is important for healing. If the patient is eating little, losing weight or has kidney disease, ask the doctor or dietitian for individual advice.
034	If the patient spends a lot of time in bed, both heels — especially the heel of the other leg — are protected from pressure with a special pillow or device chosen by the staff. Do not put anything under the leg yourself if it interferes with the prescribed leg position.
035	Smoking reduces blood flow — talk to your doctor about stopping smoking.
036	When to tell the staff at once
037	Fever, chills, sudden severe weakness, unusual drowsiness or confusion; redness or swelling spreading quickly; pus, discharge, a bad smell or darkening skin — tell the staff immediately.
038	With diabetes, even a severe infection sometimes causes no strong pain and no high fever.
039	Blackening of the toes or part of the foot, a sudden very cold foot, rapidly spreading inflammation or feeling very unwell — this needs emergency medical assessment.
040	If the patient takes insulin or tablets that can lower blood sugar, report a skipped meal, sweating, shaking, sudden weakness or confusion.
041	A hot, swollen foot without a wound
042	With diabetes and nerve damage, the foot can suddenly become red, hot and swollen — even without a wound and without strong pain. Sometimes this is damage to the bones and joints called Charcot foot.
043	Do not wait for pain: an urgent specialist examination is needed. Until then, do not put weight on that foot.
044	Before discharge, ask
045	Who will change the dressing at home, and how.
046	How much weight you may put on the affected foot, and which offloading device to use.
047	When and where the next wound check will be.
048	How to check blood sugar and take your medicines.
049	Where to turn if the wound gets worse. If the patient cannot check their feet or follow the instructions alone, arrange help from family or home-care staff in advance.
050	At home: protect both feet
051	Check your feet every day, including between the toes — with a mirror or with help from family.
052	Wash the intact skin of your feet daily in warm, not hot, water; test the temperature with your elbow or ask someone to help. Do not soak your feet in a bowl. Do not wet an open wound or a dressing without the staff's permission.
053	Dry the skin gently, especially between the toes. Moisturise dry areas, but do not put cream between the toes or on an open wound unless told to.
054	Do not walk barefoot. Check inside your shoes with your hand before putting them on. Wear socks without tight elastic.
055	Cut toenails straight across, without cutting into the corners. If nails are thick, hard to see or unsafe to cut yourself, see a podiatrist (foot-care specialist). Do not cut corns or calluses yourself and do not use corn plasters or liquids.
056	Have regular check-ups at a diabetic foot service. A new wound, redness, swelling or colour change — get it seen the same day.
