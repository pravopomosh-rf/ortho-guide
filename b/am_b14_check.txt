001	Why people fall after surgery
002	After surgery the risk of falling is higher: weakness, dizziness, painkillers and sleeping tablets, an unfamiliar room, a drip and tubes, and trips to the toilet at night. A fall can cause a new injury, so it is important to prevent it.
003	Keep the call button within reach. The first time, and whenever you have been told to get up only with help, press the button and wait for the staff.
004	Get up slowly: first sit on the edge of the bed. If you feel dizzy, lie down again and call the staff. Stand up only once the dizziness has passed, and with staff help if you have been told you need it.
005	Walk with the walking aid chosen for you; keep the walking frame or crutches next to the bed. Do not lean on the bedside cabinet, a wheeled table or the drip stand.
006	Closed shoes that fit well with non-slip soles; glasses and hearing aid if you need them; a light at night.
007	Clear rugs, cables and clutter out of the way. In the bathroom: a non-slip mat and grab rails. At night: a light on the way to the toilet. Keep your phone with you.
008	If medicines make you sleepy or dizzy, talk to your doctor or pharmacist. Do not stop them yourself.
009	Have your eyesight and hearing checked regularly.
010	When your doctor allows it, do strength and balance exercises from your physiotherapist's programme. Drink enough, unless you have a fluid restriction.
011	If you fall
012	In hospital: do not get up on your own. Press the call button or call the staff.
013	At home: if there is severe pain (especially in the operated joint), a possible injury to the head, neck, back or hip, or you cannot get up, do not try to get up on your own and call an ambulance on 101 immediately.
014	Tell your doctor about any fall.

026	Frame height
027	Stand up straight with your arms relaxed at your sides. The handgrips of the frame should be at about the level of your wrist crease. When you hold the grips, your elbows are slightly bent and your back is straight. The staff or physiotherapist will set the height.
028	How to walk
029	Move the frame a short distance ahead. All four legs should stand on level floor.
030	Before walking, ask your doctor or physiotherapist how much weight you may put on your operated leg. If weight bearing is allowed, follow the walking method you were taught. If weight bearing is not allowed, keep weight off that leg and use the walker only after training.
031	Take small steps. Turn slowly, in small steps, together with the frame.
032	Sitting down and standing up
033	Sitting down: walk up to a steady chair with armrests and turn your back to it until you feel the seat against the back of your legs. Move your hands from the frame to the armrests, one at a time, and lower yourself slowly. Do not hold on to the frame while sitting down.
034	Standing up: push up with your hands from the armrests or the seat. Do not pull the frame towards you — it can tip over. Once standing straight, take hold of the grips.
035	Clear rugs, cables and clutter; leave a wide path between rooms.
036	At night: a light on the way to the toilet.
037	In the bathroom: a non-slip mat and grab rails; if needed, a shower seat and a raised toilet seat.
038	Check the rubber tips on the frame legs: replace them if they are worn or damaged.
039	Do not use stairs or escalators with a walking frame. Your physiotherapist will show you how to manage stairs.

048	Why breathing exercises matter
049	After anaesthesia, and because of pain and lying in bed for a long time, people often breathe shallowly and cough little. Some parts of the lungs do not open up as well, and phlegm is harder to clear from the airways. This raises the risk of lung complications, including infections. Deep breathing, coughing and moving early help prevent this.
050	How to do them
051	Sit upright: in a chair, or in bed with the head raised, if this is allowed. Relax your shoulders.
052	Breathe in slowly and deeply, preferably through your nose. Hold your breath for about 3 seconds. Breathe out slowly through your mouth.
053	Take 3–5 of these breaths, then rest: more in a row can make you dizzy.
054	Then try to cough if there is phlegm. If coughing hurts, first take a deep breath in, then breathe out forcefully through an open mouth, as if steaming up a mirror. This helps move the phlegm so it is easier to cough up. Coughing does not usually damage stitches.
055	Repeat several times an hour while you are awake, or as your physiotherapist advises.
056	If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe calmly.
057	What else helps
058	Pain relief: if pain makes it hard to breathe deeply and cough, tell the nurse.
059	Moving: sit up and walk as soon as the team allows. The first time you get up, only with the staff.
060	Do not slide down the bed: a half-sitting position makes breathing easier.
061	When to tell staff at once
062	Breathlessness, chest pain, a fever, a cough with phlegm, or feeling worse — tell the nurse straight away.
063	At home: sudden breathlessness or chest pain — call an ambulance on 101. A fever or a cough with phlegm — see a doctor.

072	Swelling after surgery
073	Swelling after leg surgery is common. It is usually worse in the evening and after activity, and eases gradually over weeks and months. It is important to reduce swelling and not miss warning signs.
