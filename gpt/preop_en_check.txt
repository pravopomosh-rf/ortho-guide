=== А. ТЕКСТ ОЗВУЧКИ (ID | текст) ===
preop_en_S1 | You are scheduled for a planned operation. This video walks you through the preparation step by step: showering with antiseptic soap, medicines before surgery, fasting, which regular medicines are taken in the morning and which are stopped in advance, and what happens on the morning of surgery. We will explain why each step matters.
preop_en_S2 | Germs live on everyone's skin all the time. During surgery they can get into the wound. If an implant is placed in a bone or a joint, an infection around it is especially dangerous: sometimes another operation and a long course of antibiotics are needed. That is why the skin is prepared especially carefully before surgery.
preop_en_S3 | Many hospitals in Israel give you Septal Scrub for this — a red antiseptic soap with chlorhexidine. If your ward provides Septal Scrub and instructs you to use it, wash with it twice: the evening before surgery and on the morning of surgery. Wet your body with water. Apply about twenty-five milliliters of undiluted Septal Scrub to wet, intact skin using a sponge. Wash from your neck down to your feet, paying special attention to the surgical site, armpits, belly button, groin creases, buttocks and skin folds. Avoid the genital area, mucous membranes, open wounds and irritated skin. Use separate sponges for different parts of the body — this reduces the spread of germs from one area to another. How long to leave the lather on the skin before rinsing differs between hospitals — follow your ward's instructions. Wash your hair with regular shampoo and your face with regular soap, unless the staff tell you otherwise. Septal Scrub must not get into your eyes, ears or onto mucous membranes. If Septal Scrub gets into your eyes, rinse them immediately and thoroughly with water. If you develop eye pain, redness or changes in vision, seek medical advice promptly. Then rinse Septal Scrub off thoroughly under running water and dry yourself with a clean towel. Put on clean clothes; your bed linen should be clean too. After the shower, do not apply creams, lotions or deodorant. If you have ever had an allergic reaction to chlorhexidine, do not use Septal Scrub and tell the medical staff before washing. If you cannot wash yourself, the medical staff will wash you — in the shower or in bed.
preop_en_S4 | Usually hair does not need to be removed before surgery. Do not shave the surgical area yourself: tiny cuts from shaving are an entry point for germs. If hair is in the way, it will be removed in the hospital with a special clipper.
preop_en_S5 | The evening before surgery or in the morning, you may be given medicines to prepare you — this is called premedication. For example, Vaben, a calming medicine that reduces anxiety and may make you sleepy. Omepradex lowers the acidity of the stomach contents. It is sometimes given when there is a higher risk of aspiration — stomach contents getting into the airways. It may reduce the acid effect, but it does not prevent aspiration itself, and not every patient needs it. All these medicines are prescribed by a doctor. After a calming medicine, do not get up on your own: call the nurse to avoid a fall.
preop_en_S6 | Before anesthesia it is important to reduce the amount of food and liquid in the stomach, to lower the risk of it getting into the airways. According to the Israeli Society of Anesthesiologists, you should wait at least six hours after a light meal, and at least eight hours after a heavy or fatty meal. Many wards ask you not to eat for at least eight hours before a planned operation. If surgery is planned for the morning or early in the day, you are usually asked not to eat after midnight. If surgery is later, the staff may move the start of fasting and sometimes allow a light early breakfast. Clear liquids — for example water, or tea or coffee without milk — are allowed by general recommendations up to two hours before anesthesia starts, and chewing gum up to two hours before surgery; it must not be swallowed. But the exact limits are set by your ward or your anesthesiologist: eat, drink and chew gum only as you have been told. If you ate, drank or chewed gum after the set time, be sure to tell the medical staff. If you have diabetes, slow stomach emptying, frequent nausea or vomiting, or you take injections for weight loss or diabetes, such as Ozempic, tell the anesthesiologist in advance — special rules may apply to you.
preop_en_S7 | Fasting does not mean that all medicines are stopped. Some regular medicines should be taken in the morning with a small sip of water. These are usually heart medicines, for example beta blockers: if a dose is missed, the pulse may speed up and an arrhythmia may develop. Anti-seizure medicines: missing a dose can trigger a seizure. Medicines for Parkinson's disease: during a break, stiffness gets worse, and moving and swallowing become difficult. Eltroxin — a thyroid hormone. Asthma inhalers. Steroids, for example prednisone, if you take them regularly. Before surgery the doctor will check whether the dose needs to be changed or an extra dose given. Some medicines, on the other hand, are often skipped in the morning: for example water pills, some blood pressure medicines and diabetes tablets. Exactly which medicines to take in the morning is decided in advance by the doctor or the anesthesiologist. Bring an up-to-date list of all your medicines with their doses, and the packages themselves if you were asked to. While in hospital, do not take medicines on your own: agree on them with the medical staff.
preop_en_S8 | Eliquis, Xarelto, Pradaxa and Coumadin reduce blood clotting. Before many operations they are stopped for a while. This is especially important with spinal anesthesia: the needle is inserted into the lower part of the spinal canal, usually below the end of the spinal cord. Bleeding in this area can cause a hematoma and pressure on the nerves. The 2019 recommendations of the Israeli Society of Anesthesiologists give these guidelines: Eliquis and Xarelto — three to four days, depending on kidney function; Pradaxa — three to five days; Coumadin — four to five days, with a blood clotting test. Newer international recommendations also take the dose into account, so the final timing is set by the anesthesiologist. Sometimes, for patients at especially high risk of blood clots, the doctor prescribes enoxaparin — Clexane or Crusia — while Coumadin is paused. Compared with Coumadin, its effect is shorter and more predictable, so it is easier to plan the time of the last injection. But it depends on the dose and on kidney function. For people taking Eliquis, Xarelto or Pradaxa, such a switch is usually not needed. If you have had a stent placed in a heart artery, be sure to tell your doctor. After a stent, people often take another group of medicines — antiplatelets, for example aspirin or clopidogrel. Stopping them must be agreed with a cardiologist. The exact plan for stopping and restarting any medicine is set by the doctor. Do not change anything on your own.
preop_en_S9 | Fasting and the stress of surgery change blood sugar sharply, so diabetes medicines are adjusted before surgery. Metformin is often not taken on the day of surgery — the doctor decides this. Jardiance and Forxiga are usually stopped at least three days before: during fasting they can cause dangerous ketoacidosis, even when blood sugar is normal. Some tablets, for example gliclazide, are usually not taken on the morning of surgery: without food they can cause a dangerous drop in blood sugar. If you take Ozempic or Mounjaro injections, tell the staff in advance: they slow down stomach emptying. It is especially important to tell the doctor if you have had nausea, vomiting or a feeling of a full stomach after the injections. In hospital, tablets are often replaced with insulin around the time of surgery. Why? Insulin allows flexible control of blood sugar during fasting, surgery and recovery. The doctor plans the regimen in advance and adjusts it according to blood sugar measurements. Your blood sugar will be checked often during these days. With type 1 diabetes, basal insulin is needed even while fasting. The doctor may change its dose, but basal insulin must not be stopped completely for the whole period of surgery.
preop_en_S10 | Smoking reduces blood supply to the tissues, slows healing and increases the risk of infection. It is best to stop smoking at least four weeks before surgery. But quitting helps at any time.
preop_en_S11 | On the morning of surgery, shower with Septal Scrub if your ward has provided it and instructed you to use it. Take only the medicines you were told to take, with a small sip of water, and any prescribed premedication. Remove jewelry and piercings, remove nail polish, take out contact lenses and removable dentures, and, if the staff tell you to, your hearing aid. You will put on a hospital gown and an ID bracelet with your details. The surgeon will mark the surgical site with a marker — do not wash this mark off. Before surgery you will be asked several times to say your name, and staff will confirm which operation is planned and on which side. This is not a mistake — it is a required safety check. Before surgery, the anesthesiologist will assess your condition, explain the anesthesia plan and answer your questions. The surgeon will explain the operation, its main risks and possible alternatives in advance. After that, you sign a written informed consent — this may already be done at the pre-operative consultation. Do not hesitate to ask questions before signing.
preop_en_S12 | The key points: shower with antiseptic soap as your ward instructs, usually in the evening and in the morning; do not shave the surgical area yourself; premedication only as prescribed; fasting for the time you were told; in the morning take only the medicines the doctor allowed; blood thinners and diabetes medicines are changed in advance according to the doctor's plan. Preparing for urgent surgery, for example for a hip fracture, and for spine surgery is different — that is covered in separate videos.
preop_en_OUT | These are general recommendations. Discuss your individual situation with your treating doctor.

=== Б. ТЕКУЩАЯ EN-СТРАНИЦА (для сверки) ===
Key points in 30 seconds
Follow your fasting instructions. The ward will confirm the exact times.
Do not stop or change medicines yourself; bring a full list of them.
Shower and wash your hair the evening before. Do not shave the surgical area.

Who this leaflet is for
For patients who have a planned (elective) operation on the orthopaedic and spine surgery ward, and for their family members. This is general information: the individual instructions from your doctor, anesthesiologist, and ward are more important.
Food and drink before surgery
Before anesthesia and sedation, it is important to follow the fasting instructions you are given: this lowers the risk of stomach contents getting into the airway. The time intervals are counted back from the start of anesthesia or the procedure. If you have diabetes, slow emptying of the stomach, or other risk factors, the anesthesiologist may give you a different plan. Follow the individual instructions from the ward.
General rules for adults without increased risk (ASA, 2017):
a light meal (for example, toast and tea) — no later than 6 hours before;
fatty or fried food, meat — no later than 8 hours before;
clear liquids (water, tea or coffee without milk, clear juice without pulp) — no later than 2 hours before;
milk (not breast milk) counts as food — no later than 6 hours before.
In some cases, clear carbohydrate drinks are allowed before surgery. You should not take special pre-surgery drinks on your own: follow the written instructions from the anesthesiologist and the ward (ASA, 2023).
Medicines
Do not stop or change your medicines on your own.
Bring a full list of your medicines, including vitamins and herbal products (NICE NG5).
Get separate instructions ahead of time for each medicine you take regularly: take it in the morning, skip it for now, or change the dose. It is especially important to agree on blood pressure medicines, diabetes medicines, and medicines that affect blood clotting. Morning pills that are allowed can be taken with a small sip of water (AHA/ACC, 2024).
If you take medicines that reduce blood clotting (such as warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, clopidogrel, or aspirin), ask your doctor in advance whether any need to be stopped and when. Some, including aspirin in certain situations, may be continued. Do not stop them yourself. Your doctor will also decide whether any temporary replacement treatment is needed.
If you take empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, or canagliflozin, tell your doctor ahead of time. According to FDA labels, these are usually stopped at least 3 days before planned surgery; ertugliflozin at least 4 days. Restarting the treatment is agreed with your doctor once your condition is stable and you are eating normally again.
If you take semaglutide, tirzepatide, or other medicines for diabetes or weight loss, tell the anesthesiologist. Preparation and whether the treatment needs to be changed for a while are decided individually. Do not stop medicines on your own.
Showering and skin care before surgery
On the evening before a planned operation, take a shower with soap and wash your hair. Put on clean clothes. If possible, shower again on the morning of the surgery (Israel Ministry of Health, circular 24/2001).
What to wash with is in the section below.
Do not shave the area of the surgery: shaving raises the risk of infection. If hair needs to be removed, the hospital will do it with an electric clipper on the day of surgery (NICE NG125).
Take off jewelry, nail polish and artificial nails, and makeup.
What to wash with on the evening before and the morning of surgery
Illustration: on the left, a cleansing product with 4% chlorhexidine; on the right, wipes with 2% chlorhexidine (wipes are covered below).The ward may give you an antiseptic cleansing product with 4% chlorhexidine, for example "Septal Scrub" (Teva). If you are given it, wash with it twice: on the evening before surgery and on the morning of surgery (following the manufacturer's instructions).
How: first wash your hair with regular shampoo, and your face and outer genital area with regular soap. Rinse well. Then apply about 25 ml of the undiluted product to wet, unbroken skin and wash your body from the neck to the feet, avoiding the genitals, mucous membranes, wounds, and irritated skin. Rinse well with water, dry off with a clean towel, and put on clean clothes.
Do not let the product get into your eyes or ears, do not use it inside the nose, ears, or mouth, and do not swallow it. If it gets into your eyes, rinse them right away with plenty of water. If you have pain, redness, or vision problems, get medical help urgently.
If you have ever had an allergy to chlorhexidine or other ingredients in the product, do not use it and tell the staff. If you get itching, a rash, or redness, stop using it, rinse it off, and contact the ward or your doctor right away. If your lips or throat swell, or you have trouble breathing, call an ambulance on 101 right away. If you are already in the hospital, call the staff urgently.
If you were not given this product, take a regular shower with soap.
For use on the skin only. Keep out of reach of children.
Sources: Israel Ministry of Health — Septal Scrub package insert, 08/2024; MSKCC; NICE NG125.
Wipes with 2% chlorhexidine: who gets them and how they are used
Before some operations, for example spine surgery, staff may also clean the skin with wipes containing 2% chlorhexidine (for example, Sage, Stryker). If the patient showered first, the skin must be completely dry. If the patient cannot shower, staff help with a hygiene wash in bed and, if needed, do the pre-surgery skin cleaning separately. The ward decides according to its own protocol.

The skin is cleaned one zone after another, using a new wipe for each zone. Use back-and-forth motions, pressing firmly enough but not so hard that it hurts the skin. Pay special attention to skin folds.
Do not wipe the face, eyes, ears, mouth, genitals, mucous membranes, wounds, or damaged skin with the wipes.
After wiping, do not rinse the product off or dry it with a towel: the skin dries by itself (1–2 minutes) and may feel a little sticky — this is normal. Then put on a clean shirt and use clean sheets on the bed. Do not put on your own creams, lotions, or deodorant afterward unless the staff allow it.
If you have ever had an allergy to chlorhexidine or other ingredients in the product, do not use the wipes and tell the staff ahead of time. If you feel burning, itching, a rash, or redness, stop the cleaning and tell the staff right away. If the product gets into your eyes, rinse them right away with cool water and get medical help. If it gets into your ears or mouth, tell the staff right away so it can be rinsed out safely. If your face or throat swell, or you have trouble breathing, call the staff right away; outside the hospital, call an ambulance on 101.
Throw used wipes in the trash, not in the toilet.
Sources: DailyMed (FDA) — Sage 2% CHG Cloth; MSKCC; UCSF; University of Iowa.
What to bring with you
Bring a photo ID and the documents listed in your invitation for surgery. If the operation is arranged under a health fund (kupat holim) commitment, check ahead of time that you have a valid Form 17 with the correct medical service code. Ask at the registration desk whether this document is needed.
A list of your medicines, medical records, and test results.
Glasses, hearing aid, and dentures — in cases labeled with your name; a CPAP machine if you use one.
Comfortable clothes and closed, non-slip shoes; a walker or crutches if you were told to bring them.
It is best to leave valuables and large amounts of money at home.
ID wristband
When you are admitted, you will be given an ID wristband. Before medical procedures, staff will confirm at least two identifiers, such as your full name together with your date of birth or Israeli ID number, according to hospital rules (Israel Ministry of Health, circular 33/2006; WHO, 2007). If the details are wrong or the wristband is lost, tell the nurse right away.
Smoking and alcohol
If you smoke or drink alcohol, talk with your doctor about how to cut down or stop before surgery (NICE NG180).
When to tell the ward before surgery
Call the ward if before surgery you get a fever, a cough, a wound or rash on the skin in the area of the surgery, or if you become ill. The surgery may need to be postponed. If your life is in danger, call an ambulance on 101 (Magen David Adom).
