## P04
To prevent infection, an antibiotic is given before the operation begins. After surgery, on our ward you usually receive two or three more doses through the vein, about every eight hours, as prescribed by the doctor. To prevent blood clots, injections of enoxaparin under the skin of the abdomen — for example Clexane or Crusia — are usually started about twelve hours after surgery. The exact time of the first injection is decided by the doctor, taking into account the risk of bleeding and the type of anaesthesia. Prevention continues according to the prescribed plan, sometimes also after discharge.

## P05
On the day of your hip replacement, you stay in bed on our ward until the medical team says it is safe for you to get up. While you are in bed, the side rails are always raised. They reduce the risk of accidentally falling out of bed, but they cannot prevent every fall. Do not climb over or lower the rails yourself — it is dangerous. If you need anything or want to use the toilet, press the call button. Staff will help you or bring a urinal or bedpan. The first time you get up is usually the next day, with the physiotherapist, when it is safe to do so.

## P06
The next day a check X-ray is taken. Then the physiotherapist comes: they help you sit up, stand and take your first steps with a walker. Early movement helps recovery and lowers the risk of complications from lying in bed for a long time. But get up and walk only when it is safe, and with help from the medical team.

## P07
Sit up in bed or on a chair and do breathing exercises. You may be given a breathing exerciser: breathe in slowly and deeply through its mouthpiece, as the staff show you. This helps your lungs expand better. Breathing exercises work best together with good pain relief and early, safe movement.

## P08
Press the call button straight away if your pain suddenly gets much worse, if you have new weakness or numbness in the leg, if your foot becomes cold, if the dressing becomes very wet, if you have new pain or swelling in the calf, if it becomes hard to breathe, or if you have chest pain.

## P09
These are general recommendations. Individual instructions from your doctor and medical team come first. In the next part: discharge, rehabilitation and returning home.

## D01
After hip replacement, patients from our ward usually go to a rehabilitation hospital. There, physiotherapists help you get up, walk and manage everyday activities safely, so you are ready to return home. If you would prefer rehabilitation at home, tell the medical team in advance. This may be possible if your health and home circumstances allow it and it is approved by your health fund. A physiotherapist would then visit you at home. The advice in this video is useful both in a rehabilitation hospital and at home.

## D02
When you are discharged, you receive a discharge letter. It states which operation was done, which medicines to take and for how long, how much weight you may put on the operated leg, how to care for the wound and when to come for a follow-up visit. If you have stitches or staples that need to be removed, this is usually done after about ten to fourteen days. Some stitches dissolve by themselves. Make sure you know where to turn if problems appear at home. Show this letter to your family doctor. If anything is unclear, ask before you leave the ward.

## D03
After hip replacement, the risk of blood clots in the leg veins remains for several weeks. That is why clot prevention continues after discharge according to the prescribed plan. This may be enoxaparin injections — for example Clexane or Crusia — or tablets. Sometimes the medicines are used one after the other. Treatment often continues for about a month, sometimes longer. The doctor will tell you exactly how long. Do not skip doses and do not stop the treatment on your own — otherwise the protection against clots is reduced. But remember: these medicines increase the risk of bleeding. If you notice blood in your urine, black stools, unusually large bruises or bleeding that does not stop, get medical help urgently. Do not add aspirin, ibuprofen or other anti-inflammatory medicines on your own: some combinations increase the risk of bleeding. If you cannot give the injection yourself, arrange in advance who will help — for example a relative or the community clinic nurse.

## D04
Keep the wound clean and dry and change the dressing as you were shown. Do not put creams or ointments on the wound unless prescribed. You can usually shower with a waterproof dressing — ask the staff when and how. Do not get into a bath, a pool or the sea until the wound has fully healed and the doctor allows it: this lowers the risk of infection, and an infection around the joint replacement is a serious complication.

## D05
Take your pain medicines according to the prescribed plan: when pain is under control, it is easier to move, and movement helps recovery. If the doctor advised taking a medicine before exercises, keep to the stated time and dose. Do not take extra doses on your own. Some strong painkillers cause drowsiness, dizziness and constipation. If you have these problems, tell your doctor: drowsiness and dizziness increase the risk of falling. Swelling and bruising of the thigh and lower leg are common in the first weeks. Ice wrapped in a cloth for fifteen to twenty minutes several times a day, and resting lying down, can help. If you have been sitting for a long time, take a short walk.

## D06
The main danger in the first weeks is a fall. A fall can cause dislocation of the joint replacement or a fracture. Before you return home, prepare your home: remove rugs and cables from the floor, leave a light on at night on the way to the toilet, put the things you need at waist height. A stable chair with armrests, a raised toilet seat and grab bars in the shower are helpful. While you walk with a walker, hold it with both hands — this makes it easier to keep your balance. Small items can be carried in a bag attached to the walker, or ask someone to help. Do not try to push an ordinary trolley at the same time: it can roll away and cause a fall. At home, wear stable shoes with non-slip soles.

## D07
While the muscles and tissues around the joint are healing, some movements can cause dislocation. Which restrictions you need, and for how long, your surgeon will tell you: it depends on the surgical approach and on how the operation went. For example, after some operations you must not bend the hip a lot, cross your legs or turn the operated leg inwards for a while. These restrictions help reduce the risk of dislocation. But not everyone needs them: it all depends on the type of operation. Also ask which positions you may sleep in. Sometimes sleeping on your back, or on your side with a pillow between your knees, is recommended, to avoid a dangerous turn of the hip.

## D08
Walk every day in short walks and do the exercises the physiotherapist showed you. This helps rebuild muscle strength and lowers the risk of complications from lying in bed for a long time. But keep to the weight-bearing on the operated leg exactly as your surgeon instructed: sometimes you may put your full weight on it, and sometimes weight-bearing is limited for a while. A walker, crutches or a cane are chosen individually, and you can move to a different aid after the physiotherapist has assessed you. Use the stairs only after the physiotherapist has shown you a safe way. Hold the handrail and go one step at a time. Going up — the healthy leg first, then the operated leg and the crutch. Going down — the crutch first, then the operated leg and the healthy one. This makes it easier to keep your balance and reduces the load on the operated leg. Easy to remember: up with the healthy leg, down with the operated leg.

## D09
People usually return to driving no earlier than several weeks after surgery — when you can move your leg confidently, can brake quickly, are not taking strong painkillers and the doctor has allowed it. Short trips as a passenger are usually possible if you can get into and out of the car safely. But long journeys and flights in the first months after surgery must be discussed with your surgeon: sitting for a long time increases the risk of blood clots. If the trip is allowed, move your feet regularly and make stops to walk a little. Always tell doctors and your dentist that you have a hip replacement.

## D10
If your temperature rises to thirty-eight degrees or higher, if you have shaking chills, if the wound is getting redder and redder, or if fluid or pus comes out of it, get medical help the same day. These may be signs of infection. New pain, swelling or redness of the calf also needs an urgent check: there may be a blood clot. If you suddenly have severe pain in the hip, the leg has become shorter, is turned in an unusual way, or you cannot stand on it — do not try to walk; get emergency help immediately: there may be a dislocation or a fracture. If you suddenly become short of breath or have chest pain, call one-zero-one at once. These may be signs of a dangerous blood clot in the lungs.

## D11
Recovery can take several months. It is different for everyone: sometimes improvement comes quickly, sometimes more slowly. Come to your scheduled follow-up so the doctor can check the healing and how the new joint is working. If the operation was after a hip fracture, also ask your doctor about checking for osteoporosis and preventing falls. This helps lower the risk of a new fracture. These are general recommendations. Individual instructions from your doctor and medical team come first.
